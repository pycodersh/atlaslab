Korean fried chicken is fried twice in a thin batter, so it stays extra crispy even under sweet-spicy or soy-garlic glazes. American fried chicken usually has a thicker, seasoned crust and is fried once. Both are great, but Korean chicken is built for sharing with beer.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Korean style:** thin batter, double-fried, glazed with sauces, crispy for longer
- **American style:** thicker seasoned crust, fried once, served with biscuits or fries
- **In Korea:** chicken comes with pickled radish, and beer makes it "chimaek"
- **Avoid:** expecting ranch or gravy; Korean chicken uses its own sauces

</KeyTakeaways>

## Korean vs American fried chicken

| | Korean fried chicken | American fried chicken |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Batter** | Thin, often with potato or corn starch | Thicker flour dredge, often with buttermilk |
| **Frying** | Usually twice | Usually once |
| **Texture** | Thin, shatteringly crisp skin | Crunchy, thicker crust |
| **Flavors** | Plain, yangnyeom (sweet-spicy), soy-garlic, half-and-half | Seasoned crust, sometimes hot sauce |
| **Sides** | Pickled radish (치킨무), coleslaw, beer | Biscuits, mashed potatoes, coleslaw |
| **Portion** | Whole chicken per order, cut into pieces | By piece or bucket |

## Why Korean chicken is so crispy

Double frying is the key. The first fry cooks the chicken; after a short rest, the second fry at higher heat drives out moisture and makes the skin thin and crisp. The thin batter means less oil is absorbed, and the crispness lasts even when the chicken is coated in sauce or delivered to your door.

Korean fried chicken took off in the 1970s and 80s, and the sweet-spicy yangnyeom glaze became a national favorite. Today Korea has tens of thousands of chicken shops, many from big franchise chains.

Another difference is the setting. American fried chicken is often a quick individual meal, while Korean fried chicken is an evening event: friends gather at a chicken pub or order delivery, share a whole chicken or two, and talk over beer for hours. Chicken shops stay open late, and on big sports nights deliveries can take much longer than usual.

## Popular Korean chicken flavors

Beyond plain and yangnyeom, Korean chicken shops compete with creative flavors, and new ones appear every year.

- **Soy-garlic (간장):** sweet, salty soy glaze with garlic, a top choice for non-spicy eaters
- **Snow cheese (스노윙):** chicken dusted with sweet cheese powder
- **Green onion (파닭):** topped with shredded green onions and a tangy sauce
- **Honey butter or honey combo:** sweet glaze, popular with kids
- **Spicy (매운맛):** chili-heavy glazes for heat lovers
- **Roasted (구운 치킨):** oven-roasted instead of fried, a lighter option

Many shops also sell boneless (순살) versions of every flavor, and some offer whole fried chicken in the traditional market style (시장 통닭), fried in big pieces without batter.

## How Koreans eat fried chicken

Fried chicken in Korea is a social food: ordered by the whole chicken, shared at the table, and eaten with plastic gloves. Pickled radish cubes cut through the grease, and draft beer makes it chimaek. Delivery is huge, and chicken is a favorite on sports nights and at Han River picnics.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why is Korean fried chicken crispier?**
It's usually double-fried with a thin, starchy batter, which removes moisture and keeps the skin crisp.

**What is yangnyeom chicken?**
Fried chicken glazed with a sweet, sticky, mildly spicy gochujang-based sauce.

**Is Korean fried chicken spicy?**
Plain fried and soy-garlic aren't spicy. Yangnyeom is mild to medium, and some chains sell very hot versions.

**What do Koreans eat with fried chicken?**
Pickled radish cubes, coleslaw, and beer. Some shops add rice cakes, fries or cheese balls.
