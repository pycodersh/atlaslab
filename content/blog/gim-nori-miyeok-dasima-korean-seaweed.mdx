Korean cooking uses three main seaweeds: gim (김), thin roasted sheets for snacks and kimbap; miyeok (미역), soft wakame for soup; and dasima (다시마), thick kelp for broth. Learn which is which and you'll understand half the Korean pantry.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Gim:** thin sheets, roasted and salted for snacks or plain for kimbap
- **Miyeok:** dried wakame that expands in water; the star of birthday soup
- **Dasima:** thick dried kelp used to make savory broth
- **Avoid:** soaking too much miyeok; it expands to many times its size

</KeyTakeaways>

## The three seaweeds compared

| | Gim (김) | Miyeok (미역) | Dasima (다시마) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **What it is** | Thin pressed laver sheets | Wakame, soft and silky when soaked | Thick kelp |
| **Texture** | Crisp | Slippery, tender | Firm, leathery |

| **Flavor** | Toasty, salty (seasoned) | Mild ocean taste | Deep umami |

## How each one is used

**Gim** is everywhere: wrapped around kimbap, crumbled over rice, or eaten as seasoned gim (조미김), sheets brushed with sesame oil and salt, roasted and sold in small packs. Koreans wrap a bite of rice in a piece of seasoned gim as a simple side.

**Miyeok** is soaked in water before cooking. Its most famous dish is **miyeok-guk**, seaweed soup with beef or clams, eaten on birthdays and by new mothers after giving birth, because it's believed to be nourishing.

**Dasima** is rarely eaten on its own. It's simmered with dried anchovies to make the base broth for many soups and stews, and removed before serving. See our [anchovy stock guide](/blog/en/k-pantry/korean-anchovy-stock-guide).

- **Seasoned gim:** a popular souvenir, light and easy to pack
- **Gim bugak:** fried seaweed chips, a crunchy snack
- **Miyeok salad:** soaked wakame with vinegar dressing, a refreshing side

<Callout type="info" title="Birthday seaweed soup">
Koreans eat miyeok-guk on their birthdays, often made by a parent. Asking "Did you eat seaweed soup?" (미역국 먹었어?) is a way to ask if someone celebrated their birthday.
</Callout>

Korea is one of the world's biggest producers of gim, and seaweed farming is an important industry along the southwest coast, especially around Wando and Jindo. Seaweed is also part of everyday nutrition: it's rich in minerals and fiber, and many Korean families eat some form of seaweed almost every day, whether a sheet of gim with rice, a bowl of seaweed soup or a piece of kelp simmered into broth.

## Buying and storing seaweed

All three are sold dried and keep for months in a cool, dry place. Keep gim sealed so it stays crisp; if it softens, toast it briefly in a dry pan. A small amount of dried miyeok expands a lot when soaked, so start with less than you think. Dasima may have a white powder on its surface; that's natural umami, so wipe it gently instead of washing it off.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the difference between gim and nori?**
They're the same type of seaweed. Korean gim is often seasoned with sesame oil and salt, while Japanese nori is usually plain.

**Why do Koreans eat seaweed soup on birthdays?**
Miyeok-guk is traditionally given to new mothers for recovery, so it became a birthday dish to honor one's mother and birth.

**How do I use dasima?**
Simmer a piece in water, often with dried anchovies, to make broth, then remove it.

**Is Korean seaweed a good souvenir?**
Yes. Seasoned gim is light, cheap and popular, sold in gift sets at supermarkets and airports.
