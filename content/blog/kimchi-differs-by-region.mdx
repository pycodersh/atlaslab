Kimchi tastes different across Korea because of climate, local seafood and tradition: warmer southern regions make saltier, spicier kimchi with lots of fermented seafood, while colder northern-style kimchi is milder and more refreshing. Tasting kimchi as you travel is one of the easiest ways to notice regional food culture.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **South (Jeolla, Gyeongsang):** saltier, spicier, rich with salted seafood
- **North and central:** milder, less salty, more watery and refreshing
- **Why:** warm climates need more salt to keep kimchi from spoiling
- **Avoid:** judging all kimchi by one restaurant's version

</KeyTakeaways>

## Regional kimchi styles

| Region | Style | Taste | Typical additions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Jeolla** | Rich, deeply seasoned | Salty, spicy, complex | Lots of salted anchovy and shrimp, glutinous rice paste |
| **Gyeongsang** | Salty and spicy | Strong, punchy | Salted anchovy sauce, more chili |
| **Seoul and Gyeonggi** | Balanced | Moderate salt and spice | Salted shrimp, a balanced mix |
| **Gangwon** | Mild with seafood | Fresh, slightly briny | Squid or pollack in some kimchi |
| **Chungcheong** | Simple and mild | Less salt, light seasoning | Fewer additions |
| **Northern styles** | Watery and mild | Refreshing, less chili | Water kimchi, white kimchi traditions |

Ingredients change too. Coastal regions add fresh oysters, squid or pollack, while inland areas rely on vegetables and grains. Some southern kimchi uses a thick paste of glutinous rice to help the seasoning cling, and mountain areas sometimes add wild greens. The base vegetable can also change, from napa cabbage to radish, leafy mustard greens or green onion.

## Why kimchi changes by region

Before refrigeration, salt was the main way to keep kimchi from spoiling. Warmer southern regions used more salt and strong fermented seafood, which also made the kimchi more intense. Colder northern areas could use less salt, and their kimchi was often juicier, milder and more refreshing. Coastal regions added local seafood, while inland areas relied on more vegetables and grains.

Family recipes add another layer: every household seasons kimchi differently, and many Koreans believe their own family's kimchi is the best.

- **Jeolla kimchi** is often praised as the richest, reflecting the region's food reputation
- **Gyeongsang kimchi** is known for being salty and spicy
- **Northern-style** water kimchi is the base for cold noodle broths like naengmyeon

<Callout type="tip" title="Taste as you travel">
Kimchi is free and refillable at almost every restaurant, so it's an easy way to compare regions. Notice the saltiness, the chili level and how sour it is.
</Callout>

Many Korean families still receive kimchi from parents or grandparents in another region, and comparing family kimchi is a common topic of conversation. Some people even say they can tell where someone grew up from the kimchi they like best.

## Kimchi and the seasons

Kimchi also changes with the seasons. Spring brings fresh, lightly fermented kimchi from young vegetables. Summer is the time for cucumber kimchi and young radish kimchi, eaten fresh and cool. In late autumn, families make winter kimchi during kimjang, which ferments slowly and is eaten through the cold months.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does kimchi taste different in different regions?**
Climate, local seafood and family traditions. Warmer southern regions use more salt and seafood.

**Which region has the best kimchi?**
Jeolla kimchi is often praised as the richest, but taste is personal and every region has fans.

**Is northern kimchi less spicy?**
Generally yes. Northern-style kimchi tends to be milder and juicier, with less chili.

**Does kimchi change with the seasons?**
Yes. Fresh kimchi in spring and summer, and slow-fermented winter kimchi made during kimjang.
