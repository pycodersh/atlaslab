You ask where the restroom is, get pointed toward the door, walk out into a hallway, and find a keypad lock.

This catches almost every visitor once. Korean cafes and restaurants in multi-floor buildings usually share a restroom with the whole building, and those doors are often locked.

## Asking where it is

**화장실이 어디에 있어요?**
*hwa-jang-sil-i eo-di-e iss-eo-yo?*
"Where's the restroom?"

Shorter versions are equally fine:

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 화장실 어디예요? | hwa-jang-sil eo-di-ye-yo? | Where's the restroom? |
| 화장실이 어디에 있어요? | hwa-jang-sil-i eo-di-e iss-eo-yo? | Where is the restroom located? |
| 화장실 좀 쓸게요 | hwa-jang-sil jom sseul-ge-yo | I'm going to use the restroom |

**화장실** literally means "makeup room," which is the standard word — not a euphemism you should avoid.

## The exchange

**A:** 화장실이 어디에 있어요?  
*(Where's the restroom?)*  
**B:** 밖으로 나가서 오른쪽에 있어요.  
*(Go outside and it's on the right.)*  
**A:** 혹시 비밀번호 눌러야 하나요?  
*(Do I need to enter a code?)*  
**B:** 네, 1234예요.  
*(Yes, it's 1234.)*

> **Key Nuances**
> - **화장실**: The standard word for restroom — not a euphemism to avoid.
> - **밖으로 나가서**: "Go outside and…" — cafe restrooms are often in the shared building hallway.
> - **비밀번호 눌러야 하나요?**: Shared restrooms often have keypad locks; the code is posted inside, on your receipt, or told to you.
> - **오른쪽 / 왼쪽**: Right and left — the two direction words that carry most answers.

## The answer you'll hear

Most of the time the restroom is not inside the cafe:

**밖으로 나가서 오른쪽에 있어요**
*bakk-eu-ro na-ga-seo o-reun-jjok-e iss-eo-yo*
"Go outside and it's on the right"

Other common directions:

- **2층에 있어요** — *i-cheung-e iss-eo-yo* — "It's on the second floor"
- **복도 끝에 있어요** — *bok-do kkeut-e iss-eo-yo* — "At the end of the hallway"
- **엘리베이터 옆에 있어요** — "Next to the elevator"

Two direction words carry most of it: **오른쪽** (right) and **왼쪽** (left).

## The door code question

This is the part worth learning.

**혹시 비밀번호 눌러야 하나요?**
*hok-si bi-mil-beon-ho nul-leo-ya ha-na-yo?*
"Do I need to enter a code?"

Shared building restrooms often have keypad locks to keep out people who aren't customers. The code is usually posted inside the cafe, printed on your receipt, or simply told to you.

You'll get one of two answers:

**아니요, 그냥 들어가시면 돼요** — "No, you can just go in"

**네, 1234예요** — "Yes, it's 1234"

If they give you a code, it'll be four digits followed by the star or hash key. Press the numbers, then **별표** (star) or just push the handle.

## Getting the key instead

Some places hand you a physical key, often attached to something large so you don't walk off with it.

**열쇠 주세요** — *yeol-soe ju-se-yo* — "Key, please"

If you see a cluster of keys hanging behind the counter, that's what they're for.

## Men's and women's

Signs are usually pictures, but the words show up too:

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 남자 | nam-ja | Men |
| 여자 | yeo-ja | Women |
| 남 / 여 | nam / yeo | M / W (short form) |

In older buildings the two may share an entrance with separate stalls inside.

## One practical note

Korean restrooms often ask you to put toilet paper in a bin rather than flushing it. Newer buildings have moved away from this, but if you see a small covered bin beside the toilet with a sign, that's what it's for.

Public restrooms are common and free throughout Korea — subway stations, department stores, and large buildings all have them, and they're generally clean.

## What to actually do

1. Ask **화장실 어디예요?**
2. Expect it to be outside the cafe, in a shared hallway
3. Ask **비밀번호 눌러야 하나요?** before you walk off
4. Listen for **오른쪽** or **왼쪽** in the directions
5. If there's a keypad and no code, go back and ask

Two questions, and you avoid standing in a hallway staring at a keypad.
