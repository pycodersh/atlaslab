You can cook a lot of Korean food from an ordinary supermarket, because most home dishes are built on soy sauce, garlic, sesame oil and sugar. What you can't replace is a short list of fermented staples, and those are easy to order online.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Buy anywhere:** soy sauce, garlic, toasted sesame oil, short-grain rice, tofu, eggs, everyday vegetables
- **Workable swaps:** paprika plus cayenne for gochugaru, apple for Korean pear, shiso for perilla
- **Order online:** gochujang, gochugaru and, if you like stews, doenjang
- **Avoid:** sriracha for gochujang or sauerkraut for kimchi; they don't taste Korean

</KeyTakeaways>

## Supermarket swaps that work (and ones that don't)

| Korean ingredient | Supermarket swap | How close |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Soy sauce (jin-ganjang)** | Japanese soy sauce | Very close |
| **Short-grain rice** | Sushi rice | Very close |
| **Gochugaru** | Sweet paprika + a pinch of cayenne | Workable bridge |
| **Korean pear (marinades)** | Grated apple or Bosc pear | Workable |
| **Perilla leaf** | Shiso, or lettuce just for wrapping | Workable |
| **Soup soy sauce** | Regular soy sauce + a pinch of salt | Workable |
| **Rice syrup** | Honey or corn syrup, a little less | Workable |
| **Doenjang** | Red miso + a splash of soy sauce | Close, but not the same |
| **Gochujang** | Sriracha, harissa | Doesn't work |
| **Kimchi** | Sauerkraut | Doesn't work |

## What you can already buy anywhere

Garlic, onions, green onions, carrots, zucchini, potatoes, eggs and tofu are the same everywhere, and Korean home cooking uses them constantly. Sushi rice is the same short-grain type Koreans eat. A Japanese soy sauce works for most recipes; Korean soup soy sauce mainly matters in clear soups. For sesame oil, check the label says toasted. The pale, untoasted kind has almost no flavor.

That short list already covers bulgogi, japchae (with any glass or rice noodles you can find), seasoned spinach, steamed egg and soy-braised potatoes. Those dishes never needed a specialty store in the first place.

<Callout type="tip" title="Read the label for 'toasted'">
Korean food leans heavily on toasted sesame oil. If the bottle is pale yellow and smells faintly of nothing, it's untoasted. The right one is deep amber and smells strongly of roasted sesame.
</Callout>

## The three that don't substitute

**Gochujang.** Recipes online suggest sriracha, harissa or miso with chili. None of them combine fermented soybean, chili and rice starch, so none of them taste like gochujang. If you plan to cook Korean food more than occasionally, this is the jar to order first. See our [gochujang substitutes guide](/blog/en/k-pantry/gochujang-substitutes) for the mixes that come closest when you're stuck.

**Doenjang.** Red miso is the nearest option and makes a good soup, but doenjang is saltier, coarser and funkier. A miso stew is pleasant; it just isn't doenjang jjigae.

**Kimchi.** Sauerkraut is fermented cabbage, but the seasoning, texture and flavor are completely different. Fortunately kimchi is now sold in many mainstream supermarkets, and it's also the easiest of the three to make yourself.

## The minimum online order

If you order anything, start with three items:

1. **Gochujang:** one tub lasts months in the fridge.
2. **Gochugaru:** coarse grind, stored in the freezer.
3. **Doenjang:** optional if you already have red miso.

Together they usually cost under 30 dollars and unlock most Korean home recipes, from tteokbokki and kimchi stew to spicy pork and bibimbap. Everything else on long "Korean pantry essentials" lists can wait until you know which dishes you actually cook.

## What to cook first

Start with dishes that were never going to need a substitute: bulgogi, japchae, seasoned spinach (sigeumchi namul), steamed egg (gyeran-jjim) and soy-braised potatoes (gamja jorim). Cook those while your order arrives, then add the gochujang-based dishes. Building up this way keeps you cooking instead of waiting for a perfect pantry.

![Japchae](https://eecvvgkihtcgfikaimao.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/pantry-recipe-images/japchae-done.png)

When you do find a Korean or Asian grocer, add items one at a time: dried anchovies and kelp for stock, glass noodles (dangmyeon), and frozen dumplings are the most useful next steps.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I cook Korean food with regular supermarket ingredients?**
Yes. Bulgogi, japchae, namul, steamed egg and soy-braised dishes need only standard ingredients.

**What Korean ingredient should I order online first?**
Gochujang, then gochugaru. Together they unlock most spicy Korean dishes.

**Can I use miso instead of doenjang?**
For everyday stews, yes, with a splash of soy sauce. It won't taste exactly like doenjang jjigae.

**Is there a substitute for kimchi?**
Not really. Buy it at a supermarket or make a simple batch at home.
