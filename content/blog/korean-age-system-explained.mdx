Since June 2023, Korea officially uses international age (만 나이) for legal and administrative purposes, but you'll still hear the traditional "Korean age" and "year age" in daily life. Age matters socially because it decides who speaks politely to whom, so expect to be asked how old you are or what year you were born.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Official since 2023:** international age (만 나이), counted from birth like most countries
- **Traditional Korean age:** you're 1 at birth and gain a year every January 1
- **Year age (연 나이):** current year minus birth year; still used for some rules like school years
- **Avoid:** being surprised when someone asks your age early on; it's how Koreans set speech levels

</KeyTakeaways>

## The three ways to count age

| System | Korean | How it works | Where it's used |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **International age** | 만 나이 | Age from birth, +1 on your birthday | Official default since June 2023 |
| **Korean age** | 세는 나이 | 1 at birth, +1 every January 1 | Traditional, still heard socially |
| **Year age** | 연 나이 | Current year − birth year | Some laws, school years, military service |

## Quick calculation

Example: someone born in October 2000, on a date in June 2026.

| System | Calculation | Age |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **International age** | Birthday not yet passed this year | 25 |
| **Year age** | 2026 − 2000 | 26 |
| **Korean age** | Year age + 1 | 27 |

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask by birth year">
Because of the different systems, many Koreans ask "What year were you born?" (몇 년생이에요?) instead of "How old are you?" It avoids confusion and quickly sets who's older.
</Callout>

## Why age matters socially

| Situation | What changes |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Speech level** | Younger people use polite speech (존댓말) with older people |
| **Titles** | 언니 · 오빠 · 누나 · 형 for slightly older friends, depending on your gender |
| **Same age (동갑)** | Friends born the same year often switch to casual speech (반말) |
| **At work** | Rank usually matters more than age |
| **Paying** | Older people often pay for meals |

## The "early-born" puzzle

Before the school system changed, children born in January and February often started school a year early, alongside classmates born the previous year. These "빠른 년생" (*ppareun nyeonsaeng*, early-born) people may identify with the older group, which can make working out who's older a little complicated. Today it mostly matters for people born before the late 2000s, and many Koreans laugh about the confusion.

| Born | Usually grouped with | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **March–December 1998** | The 1998 cohort | Standard |
| **January–February 1999 (early-born)** | Often the 1998 school cohort | May call 1998-born friends by name |
| **March 1999 onward** | The 1999 cohort | Standard |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Korea still use Korean age?**
Officially, no. Since June 2023, international age is the standard for laws and documents, though some people still use Korean age in conversation.

**What is year age used for?**
Some rules use year age, such as school entry and certain youth protection and military rules.

**Why do Koreans ask my age?**
Age decides speech levels and how people address each other, so it helps them speak to you appropriately.

**What does 동갑 mean?**
Born in the same year. Friends who are 동갑 usually speak casually with each other.
