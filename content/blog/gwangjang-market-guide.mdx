Gwangjang Market opened in 1905 and is one of Seoul's oldest markets. Its central food alley is a grid of stalls where you sit on a bench and eat what's cooked in front of you. Point, sit, eat, pay: it's easier than it looks.

<KeyTakeaways title="At a glance">

- **Nearest stations:** Jongno 5-ga (Line 1) and Euljiro 4-ga (Lines 2 and 5), each a few minutes' walk
- **How long:** 1 to 2 hours to eat and wander
- **Cost:** no entry fee; plan on a few dishes per person at a few thousand to 25,000 won each
- **Booking:** none; just find a seat
- **Hours:** the food alley runs from late morning into the evening; stalls set their own hours and some close on Sundays

</KeyTakeaways>

## Getting there

Search **광장시장** in Naver Map or Kakao Map rather than an English name; both show the market entrances and the walk from the station exits. From Jongno 5-ga Station, the market is on the south side of the main road. The food alley is in the middle of the market, where the cross-shaped aisles meet. The [map apps guide](/blog/en/k-patto/google-maps-alternatives-korea) explains why Google Maps walking directions are unreliable in Korea.

## What to eat

| Dish | What it is | Rough price |
|---|---|---|
| **빈대떡** bindaetteok | crisp mung bean pancake, best with makgeolli | 5,000–7,000 won |
| **마약김밥** mayak gimbap | mini rice rolls with mustard dip | 3,000–5,000 won a plate |
| **육회** yukhoe | seasoned raw beef with pear and egg yolk | 15,000–25,000 won |
| **꽈배기** kkwabaegi | chewy twisted doughnut | 1,000–2,000 won |
| **칼국수** kalguksu | hand-cut noodle soup | 7,000–9,000 won |

Prices change by stall and season, so check the board before ordering. The best-known yukhoe and kalguksu shops have the longest lines; bindaetteok and gimbap stalls usually move quickly. Portions have been criticized in the past, so a glance at what your neighbors are eating tells you what to expect.

## Paying

| Method | Works? | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | everywhere | keep 1,000 and 10,000 won notes handy |
| Card | most stalls now | ask **카드 돼요?** before you order |
| Bank transfer | common with locals | needs a Korean bank account |

## How to order at a stall

1. **Pick a stall** by what's on display and what locals are eating.
2. **Sit where the vendor points.** Sitting at a stall means ordering from that stall; don't bring food from elsewhere.
3. **Order one or two dishes at a time**, then add more.
4. **Pay when you finish.**

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 빈대떡 하나 주세요. | bindaetteok hana juseyo | One mung bean pancake, please. |
| 카드 돼요? | kadeu dwaeyo? | Do you take card? |
| 포장해 주세요. | pojanghae juseyo | To go, please. |

You'll hear **여기 앉으세요** ("sit here") as the vendor waves you in. The [traditional market guide](/blog/en/k-patto/shopping-at-a-korean-traditional-market) has more market Korean, including how to ask for a better price.

## Beyond the food alley

The halls around the food alley sell silk, hanbok fabric and bedding, and the upper floor of the east building has vintage clothing shops popular with younger visitors. A walk through them after eating shows the market's everyday side.

<Callout type="tip" title="Timing">

Weekend lunchtimes are the most crowded. Weekday afternoons are calmer, and early evening is when the bindaetteok and makgeolli crowd arrives.

</Callout>

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When is Gwangjang Market open?**
The food alley is busiest from late morning into the evening. Stalls set their own hours and some close on Sundays, so go earlier in the day for the widest choice.

**Is yukhoe safe to eat?**
Yukhoe is made from fresh raw beef at specialist shops with high turnover. If you're pregnant or have a sensitive stomach, stick to cooked dishes.

**Is Gwangjang Market a tourist trap?**
It's popular with tourists, and some prices are higher than at neighborhood markets. Comparing a few stalls and checking prices first keeps it good value.

**Can I take food away?**
Yes. Most stalls pack bindaetteok, gimbap and doughnuts to go; ask for 포장 (*pojang*).
