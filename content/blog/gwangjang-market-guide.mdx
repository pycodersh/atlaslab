Gwangjang Market is Seoul's oldest traditional market, and its central food alley is packed with stalls where you sit on a bench and eat what's cooked in front of you. Bring some cash, point at what looks good, and squeeze onto the bench like everyone else.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Getting there:** Jongno 5-ga Station (Line 1) or Euljiro 4-ga Station (Lines 2 and 5)
- **Budget:** around 15,000–30,000 KRW per person for three or four dishes
- **Payment:** cards are increasingly accepted, but carry cash as backup
- **Avoid:** weekend lunch hours if you hate crowds; weekday afternoons are calmer

</KeyTakeaways>

## The must-eats compared

| Dish | Korean | What it is | Rough price | Wait | Taste |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bindaetteok** | 빈대떡 | Mung bean pancake, fried crisp | 5,000–7,000 KRW | Short to medium; famous stalls queue | Nutty, crunchy, best with makgeolli |
| **Mayak gimbap** | 마약김밥 | Mini rice rolls with mustard sauce | 3,000–5,000 KRW a plate | Usually short | Simple, salty, moreish |
| **Yukhoe** | 육회 | Seasoned raw beef with pear and egg yolk | 15,000–25,000 KRW | Long at the best-known shops | Sweet, nutty, silky |
| **Chapssal kkwabaegi** | 찹쌀꽈배기 | Twisted glutinous-rice doughnut | 1,000–2,000 KRW each | Short | Chewy, sugary |
| **Kalguksu** | 칼국수 | Hand-cut noodle soup | 7,000–9,000 KRW | Medium at the Netflix-famous stall | Mild, comforting |

Prices vary by stall and change often, so check the board before ordering. Portion sizes have been a sore point in the past; a quick look at what neighbors are eating tells you what to expect.

## Paying: cash, card or transfer

| Method | Works? | Tip |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cash** | Everywhere | Keep 1,000 and 10,000 KRW notes handy |
| **Card** | Most stalls now | Ask 카드 돼요? before ordering |
| **Bank transfer (계좌이체)** | Common with locals | Needs a Korean bank account, so not practical for most visitors |

<Callout type="tip" title="Getting a seat">
Stalls have benches around a counter. Stand near the stall you want, catch the vendor's eye, and they'll point you to a gap. Sitting at a stall means ordering from that stall; don't bring food from elsewhere.
</Callout>

## Beyond the food alley

Gwangjang is a working market as well as a food destination. The surrounding halls sell silk, hanbok fabric, bedding and vintage clothing, and the second floor of the eastern building is known for secondhand and vintage shops popular with younger visitors. Walking through them after eating is a good way to see the market's everyday side.

| Area | What you'll find | Good for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Central food alley** | Bindaetteok, gimbap, yukhoe, noodles | Eating |
| **Fabric and hanbok halls** | Silk, traditional fabrics, tailors | A glimpse of the market's history |
| **Vintage floor** | Secondhand jackets, sweaters, accessories | Browsing and bargain hunting |

## How to order at a stall

1. **Pick a stall** by looking at what's on display and what locals are eating.
2. **Sit when there's space**; the vendor will wave you in.
3. **Point and order**, one or two dishes at a time.
4. **Pay when you finish**, in cash or by card.

- Check prices on the board before ordering

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When is Gwangjang Market open?**
The food alley is busiest from late morning into the evening. Individual stalls set their own hours, and some close on Sundays, so go earlier in the day for the widest choice.

**Is yukhoe safe to eat?**
Yukhoe is made from fresh raw beef at specialist shops with high turnover. If you're pregnant or have a sensitive stomach, stick to cooked dishes.

**Is Gwangjang Market a tourist trap?**
It's popular with tourists, and some prices are higher than at neighborhood markets. Comparing a few stalls and checking prices first keeps it good value.

**Can I take food away?**
Yes. Most stalls pack bindaetteok, gimbap and doughnuts to go; ask for 포장 (*pojang*).
