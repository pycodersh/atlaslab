Miso can stand in for doenjang in an everyday Korean stew, but not as a straight one-to-one swap. Doenjang is saltier, funkier and made to be boiled, so miso needs a little extra salt, a stronger miso variety and a different point in the cooking.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Doenjang:** 100% soybeans and salt, wild-fermented, chunky, pungent, boil-friendly
- **Miso:** soybeans plus rice or barley, koji-fermented, smooth, milder, sweeter
- **Swap rule:** red or mixed miso, plus a splash of soy sauce, stirred in at the end
- **Avoid:** white miso in Korean stews; it's far too sweet and mild

</KeyTakeaways>

## Doenjang vs miso at a glance

| | Korean doenjang (된장) | Japanese miso (味噌) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ingredients** | Soybeans and salt brine | Soybeans, rice or barley, salt |
| **Fermentation** | Wild bacteria on dried soybean blocks (meju) | Controlled koji mold |
| **Texture** | Coarse, with soybean bits | Smooth paste |
| **Flavor** | Salty, earthy, strongly fermented | Milder, rounder, sweeter |
| **Aging** | Months to years | Weeks to months (red miso longer) |
| **Under heat** | Gets deeper with long boiling | Aroma fades when boiled |

## Why they taste so different

Traditional doenjang starts with *meju* (메주): boiled, mashed soybeans shaped into blocks and left to dry and ferment with whatever microbes are in the air. The blocks then soak in salt brine in earthenware jars (옹기). The liquid becomes soup soy sauce, and the solids become doenjang, which is dense, salty and pungent.

Miso adds a grain, usually rice or barley, and is inoculated with a single cultured mold called koji. The grain gives sweetness, and the controlled ferment gives a cleaner, gentler taste. Both are excellent; they just do different jobs. Korean stews are built around doenjang's boldness, so plain miso tastes flat and slightly sweet in a doenjang jjigae unless you adjust for it.

<Callout type="tip" title="The three-part fix">
Use red miso (aka) or mixed miso (awase), add up to ½ tsp soy sauce per tablespoon of miso for the missing salt, and add a little extra garlic and a pinch of gochugaru to cut the sweetness.
</Callout>

## Making doenjang jjigae with miso

Doenjang usually goes into the pot early and boils with everything else. With miso, change the order so its flavor isn't cooked away.

1. **Build the broth.** Bring anchovy-kelp stock or water to a boil in a pot or ttukbaegi (earthenware pot). Don't add the paste yet.
2. **Cook the firm ingredients.** Add diced potato, zucchini, onion and pork or seafood. Boil 10–15 minutes until the potato is tender.
3. **Season the broth.** Add ½ tsp soy sauce or fish sauce, 1 tbsp minced garlic and 1 tsp gochugaru. Boil one more minute.
4. **Add the miso off the heat.** Turn off the burner. Use the same amount of miso as the recipe's doenjang and dissolve it through a small sieve so it blends smoothly.
5. **Finish.** Stir in tofu, green onion and sliced green chili. The heat of the broth warms them without boiling the miso.

If you have both pastes, a half-and-half mix is a good compromise. It keeps doenjang's depth while softening the funk for people new to it.

## When miso won't work

Some Korean dishes depend on doenjang's texture or its long boil, and miso falls short.

- **Ssamjang:** the dipping sauce for lettuce wraps needs doenjang's thick, chunky body. Miso makes it runny and mild.
- **Long-boiled pork dishes:** boiled pork (bossam) and pork bone stew (gamjatang) cook with doenjang for an hour or more to remove gamey smells. Miso loses its flavor and can turn bitter.
- **Gang-doenjang:** a thick, concentrated doenjang stew mixed into rice. It needs strongly aged paste.

For these, buy a tub of Korean doenjang from an Asian grocer or online. It keeps two years or more in the fridge, so one tub lasts a long time.

## Buying doenjang

Supermarket doenjang in Korea ranges from mild, smooth factory-made tubs to coarse traditional paste labeled 재래식 된장 (traditional-style doenjang). Factory doenjang is the easier start. Traditional paste is stronger and saltier, so use a little less. You'll also see ssamjang, a ready-mixed blend of doenjang and gochujang for wraps. It's handy, but it's not a replacement for plain doenjang in stews.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I use miso instead of doenjang?**
Yes, in everyday stews. Use red or mixed miso, add a little soy sauce, and stir it in at the end.

**What's the main difference between doenjang and miso?**
Doenjang is pure soybean and wild-fermented, so it's saltier and funkier. Miso includes grain and koji, so it's milder and sweeter.

**Can I use white miso for doenjang jjigae?**
It's not recommended. White miso is too sweet and mild for Korean stews.

**Does doenjang go bad?**
It keeps two years or more in the fridge. Darkening over time is normal.
