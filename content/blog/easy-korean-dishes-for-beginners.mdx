You don't need a Korean grocery store or special equipment to start cooking Korean food. These five dishes use a handful of pantry staples, take under 40 minutes, and teach the flavors you'll use again and again.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Start with:** kimchi fried rice, the easiest and most forgiving
- **Pantry basics:** gochujang, soy sauce, sesame oil, garlic and kimchi cover most dishes
- **Equipment:** a frying pan, a small pot and a rice cooker or saucepan
- **Avoid:** using fresh kimchi for cooked dishes; older, sour kimchi tastes better

</KeyTakeaways>

## Five beginner dishes

| Dish | Korean | Time | Difficulty | Key ingredients |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Kimchi fried rice** | 김치볶음밥 | 15 min | Very easy | Kimchi, cooked rice, egg, sesame oil |
| **Steamed egg** | 계란찜 | 10 min | Very easy | Eggs, water or stock, salt, green onion |
| **Bulgogi** | 불고기 | 30 min + marinating | Easy | Thin beef, soy sauce, sugar, garlic, pear |

| **Bibimbap** | 비빔밥 | 40 min | Medium | Rice, vegetables, egg, gochujang |

## Why these five?

Together they cover the core Korean techniques: stir-frying, steaming, marinating, simmering and seasoning vegetables. **Kimchi fried rice** teaches how kimchi transforms a dish. **Gyeran-jjim** shows how gentle heat makes soft, savory egg. **Bulgogi** introduces the sweet soy marinade used across Korean cooking. **Kimchi jjigae** is the everyday stew most Koreans grow up with, and **bibimbap** brings together seasoned vegetables, rice and gochujang.

- **Shopping list:** gochujang, gochugaru, soy sauce, sesame oil, garlic, green onion, eggs, rice, kimchi
- **Make-ahead:** bulgogi marinade and bibimbap vegetables keep in the fridge for a few days
- **Leftovers:** leftover rice is perfect for kimchi fried rice the next day

<Callout type="tip" title="Day-old rice works best">
For fried rice, use cooked rice that has cooled, ideally from the day before. Fresh, hot rice turns mushy in the pan.
</Callout>

## Building your skills

Once you're comfortable with these five, you can branch out with the same pantry. Doenjang jjigae uses the same method as kimchi jjigae with soybean paste instead. Japchae builds on bulgogi's soy marinade. Namul side dishes use the seasoning you learned for bibimbap toppings, and tteokbokki needs only rice cakes, gochujang, sugar and fish cakes.

A few habits make Korean cooking easier:

- **Prep first:** chop vegetables and mix sauces before turning on the heat
- **Taste often:** gochujang, soy sauce and doenjang vary in saltiness by brand
- **Cook rice first:** start the rice before the rest of the meal so everything finishes together
- **Make banchan ahead:** a few namul or a batch of braised potatoes last several days in the fridge

## A simple first meal

Start with kimchi fried rice. Fry chopped kimchi in a little oil for a few minutes, add cold rice, a spoon of gochujang or soy sauce, and stir-fry until hot. Finish with sesame oil, top with a fried egg, and sprinkle with seaweed flakes and sesame seeds. Serve it with a quick gyeran-jjim for a two-dish Korean dinner.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the easiest Korean dish to cook?**
Kimchi fried rice: kimchi, leftover rice and an egg, ready in 15 minutes.

**What ingredients do I need to start cooking Korean food?**
Gochujang, gochugaru, soy sauce, sesame oil, garlic, green onion, rice and kimchi.

**Do I need a rice cooker?**
It helps, but a saucepan with a lid works fine for cooking rice.

**Can I cook Korean food without a Korean store?**
Many dishes can be made with substitutes. See our guide to [cooking without a Korean grocery store](/blog/en/k-pantry/cook-korean-food-without-korean-grocery-store).
