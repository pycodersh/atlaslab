People say you can learn to read Korean in a day, and unlike most language-learning claims, this one is close to true. Not because Korean is easy — it is not — but because Hangul was deliberately engineered in the 1440s to be learnable by people who had no time for study.

The whole system is 24 basic letters and three rules for stacking them. That is genuinely a day's work.

What follows is the order that works. Skip ahead and you will get stuck; follow it and you will be sounding out signs by evening.

## Step 1: Six vowels, then four more

Start with the six that everything else is built from:

| Letter | Sound |
|---|---|
| ㅏ | *a* as in father |
| ㅓ | *eo* — like "uh", lips relaxed |
| ㅗ | *o* — lips rounded |
| ㅜ | *u* as in food |
| ㅡ | *eu* — lips spread, no rounding |
| ㅣ | *i* as in machine |

The shapes are not arbitrary. A vertical line means the vowel is written to the right of a consonant. A horizontal line means it goes underneath. You will use this in Step 3.

Now add a second stroke to four of them and you get the *y* versions:

ㅏ → ㅑ (*ya*) · ㅓ → ㅕ (*yeo*) · ㅗ → ㅛ (*yo*) · ㅜ → ㅠ (*yu*)

That is ten vowels, and you learned four of them for free.

## Step 2: Fourteen consonants

| Letter | Sound |
|---|---|
| ㄱ | g / k |
| ㄴ | n |
| ㄷ | d / t |
| ㄹ | between r and l |
| ㅁ | m |
| ㅂ | b / p |
| ㅅ | s |
| ㅇ | silent at the start |
| ㅈ | j |
| ㅊ | ch |
| ㅋ | k with a puff of air |
| ㅌ | t with a puff of air |
| ㅍ | p with a puff of air |
| ㅎ | h |

Two things make this faster than it looks.

First, the shapes copy the mouth. ㄱ is the back of the tongue rising. ㄴ is the tongue tip touching behind the teeth. ㅁ is a mouth. ㅅ is teeth. ㅇ is the open throat.

Second, the last four are just earlier letters with one extra stroke, and that stroke always means "add a puff of air": ㄱ→ㅋ, ㄷ→ㅌ, ㅂ→ㅍ, ㅈ→ㅊ.

**ㅇ deserves special attention.** At the start of a syllable it makes no sound at all — it is a placeholder, because every Korean syllable must begin with a consonant position. 아 is just *a*. At the bottom of a syllable, the same letter becomes *ng*.

## Step 3: How letters become syllables

Korean does not write letters in a line. It packs them into square blocks, one block per syllable. There are three shapes, and which one you use depends on the vowel.

**Vertical vowel → side by side**

ㄱ + ㅏ = 가 · ㄴ + ㅣ = 니 · ㅁ + ㅓ = 머

**Horizontal vowel → stacked**

ㄱ + ㅗ = 고 · ㅁ + ㅜ = 무 · ㅅ + ㅡ = 스

**Add a final consonant → it goes underneath**

가 + ㅁ = 감 · 고 + ㄱ = 곡 · 무 + ㄹ = 물

That final consonant is called [*batchim*](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-batchim-final-consonants-explained), and it is the only part of Hangul that takes longer than a day to get comfortable with. For now, just know it sits at the bottom.

## Step 4: Read something

Try these out loud before reading the answers.

- 우유
- 가구
- 나무
- 김치
- 비빔밥

*u-yu* (milk) · *ga-gu* (furniture) · *na-mu* (tree) · *gim-chi* · *bi-bim-bap*

If you got most of those, you can read Hangul. Everything after this is refinement.

## What to skip on day one

Three things will slow you down if you try to learn them now.

[**Tense consonants**](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-consonants-19-basic-sounds) (ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ) — doubled letters with a sharper, tighter sound. Learn them on day two.

**Complex vowels** (ㅐ ㅔ ㅘ ㅢ and friends) — combinations of what you already know. They come easily once the basics are automatic.

**Sound change rules** — Korean pronunciation does not always match the spelling. 한국말 is said *han-gung-mal*. This is real and important, but it is a listening problem, not a reading problem, and trying to learn it on day one is what makes people quit.

## A realistic practice plan

Reading Hangul is a recognition skill, so short and frequent beats long and thorough. Twenty minutes of sounding out real words beats two hours of staring at a chart.

Subway station signs, food packaging, and K-pop song titles all work well because you often already know what the word should be, which lets you check yourself instantly.
