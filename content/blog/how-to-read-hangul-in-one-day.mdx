Learning to read Korean in a day is one of the few language-learning claims that's close to true. Hangul was designed in the 1440s to be learned quickly: 24 basic letters and three ways to stack them. The order below is what makes it work.

  <RuleAvoid ko="ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ" rom="tense consonants" note="Trying to learn every letter on day one." warn="Save tense consonants and complex vowels for day two." />

    <RuleExample ko="가 + ㅁ = 감" rom="gam" en="A final consonant goes underneath" />

  </RuleInstead>

## Step 1: six vowels, then four more

| Letter | Sound | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| ㅏ | *a* | as in "father" |
| ㅓ | *eo* | like "uh", lips relaxed |
| ㅗ | *o* | lips rounded |
| ㅜ | *u* | as in "food" |
| ㅡ | *eu* | lips spread, no rounding |
| ㅣ | *i* | as in "machine" |

A vertical line means the vowel sits to the right of a consonant; a horizontal line means it goes underneath. You'll use that in Step 3.

Add a second stroke and you get the *y*-versions: ㅏ → ㅑ (*ya*), ㅓ → ㅕ (*yeo*), ㅗ → ㅛ (*yo*), ㅜ → ㅠ (*yu*). That's ten vowels, four of them free. The [vowels guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-vowels-all-21-explained) covers the rest.

## Step 2: fourteen consonants

| Letter | Sound | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| ㄱ ㄴ ㄷ | g/k · n · d/t | the softer sound at the start |
| ㄹ | r/l | a light tap, not an English R |
| ㅁ ㅂ ㅅ | m · b/p · s | |
| ㅇ | silent / ng | silent at the start, *ng* at the bottom |
| ㅈ ㅊ | j · ch | |
| ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ | k · t · p | with a puff of air |
| ㅎ | h | |

Two things make this faster than it looks. The shapes copy the mouth: ㄱ is the back of the tongue rising, ㄴ the tongue tip behind the teeth, ㅁ a mouth, ㅅ a tooth, ㅇ the open throat. And one extra stroke always means "add a puff of air": ㄱ → ㅋ, ㄷ → ㅌ, ㅂ → ㅍ, ㅈ → ㅊ. The [consonants guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-consonants-19-basic-sounds) explains the full system.

**ㅇ is the one to watch.** At the start of a syllable it makes no sound; it just fills the consonant slot. 아 is *a*. At the bottom of a syllable it's *ng*: 앙 is *ang*.

## Step 3: letters into syllable blocks

Korean doesn't write letters in a line. It packs them into one square block per syllable, and the vowel decides the shape.

| Layout | Examples | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical vowel: side by side | 가 · 니 · 머 | *ga · ni · meo* |
| Horizontal vowel: stacked | 고 · 무 · 스 | *go · mu · seu* |
| Final consonant: underneath | 감 · 곡 · 물 | *gam · gok · mul* |

That bottom consonant is called [batchim](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-batchim-final-consonants-explained). It's the only part of Hangul that takes longer than a day to feel comfortable. For now, just know it sits at the bottom. The [syllable structure guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-syllable-structure-why-every-block-matters) shows every layout.

## Step 4: read something

Try these out loud before checking.

| Word | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 우유 | *uyu* | milk |
| 가구 | *gagu* | furniture |
| 나무 | *namu* | tree |
| 김치 | *gimchi* | kimchi |
| 비빔밥 | *bibimbap* | bibimbap |

If you got most of those, you can read Hangul. Everything after this is refinement.

## What to skip on day one

- **Tense consonants** (ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ): doubled letters with a tighter sound. Day two.
- **Complex vowels** (ㅐ ㅔ ㅘ ㅢ…): combinations of what you already know.
- **Sound changes:** Korean isn't always said the way it's spelled. 한국말 is said *hangungmal*. That's a listening skill, not a reading one, and trying to learn it on day one is what makes people quit.

<Callout type="tip" title="How to practice">

Short and frequent beats long. Twenty minutes of sounding out real words does more than two hours with a chart. Subway signs, food packaging and K-pop titles work well, because you often already know what the word should be and can check yourself.

</Callout>
