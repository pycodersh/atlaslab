Korean kitchens keep up to three soy sauces: jin-ganjang for braising and stir-frying, guk-ganjang for soups, and yangjo-ganjang for dipping and dressings. If you buy only one, choose jin-ganjang; it handles most everyday Korean cooking.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Jin-ganjang:** all-purpose, slightly sweet, for braises, marinades and stir-fries
- **Guk-ganjang:** soup soy sauce, lighter in color but saltier
- **Yangjo-ganjang:** naturally brewed, rich flavor, best for dipping and dressings
- **Avoid:** using guk-ganjang like regular soy sauce; it's much saltier

</KeyTakeaways>

## The three types compared

| Type | Korean | Color | Taste | Best for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Jin-ganjang** | 진간장 | Dark | Balanced, slightly sweet | Bulgogi, jorim, stir-fries, marinades |
| **Guk-ganjang** | 국간장 | Light | Very salty, earthy | Soups, namul seasoning |
| **Yangjo-ganjang** | 양조간장 | Medium-dark | Rich, rounded, brewed | Dipping sauces, dressings, raw dishes |

## Why the difference matters

**Guk-ganjang** (soup soy sauce) is made traditionally from fermented soybean blocks, the same process that produces doenjang. It's light in color so soups stay clear, but it's saltier than regular soy sauce. **Jin-ganjang** is a mass-produced, all-purpose soy sauce that holds up to heat, which is why it's used for braised and caramelized dishes. **Yangjo-ganjang** is naturally brewed over months, giving a fuller flavor that shines when uncooked.

- **Label tip:** look for 양조 (brewed) or 혼합 (blended) on the bottle
- **Sweetness:** jin-ganjang often contains a little sweetener; check if you're watching sugar
- **Japanese soy sauce:** works as a substitute for jin-ganjang or yangjo-ganjang

<Callout type="tip" title="One bottle to start">
Starting out? Buy jin-ganjang or a good brewed soy sauce for most cooking, then add guk-ganjang later if you make a lot of Korean soups and namul.
</Callout>

## Soy sauce in Korean food culture

Soy sauce has been made in Korea for centuries, and traditional households once brewed their own in large earthenware jars (옹기) on a terrace called jangdokdae (장독대). Fermented soybean blocks (메주) were soaked in brine for months; the liquid became soup soy sauce, and the solids became doenjang. Some old families still keep soy sauce that has aged for many years, prized for its deep flavor.

Today most people buy soy sauce at the supermarket, but the different types still reflect that tradition. Many Korean recipes specify which soy sauce to use, so the label matters.

- **Sauce ratios:** a common marinade base is soy sauce, sugar, garlic and sesame oil
- **Dipping sauce:** soy sauce, vinegar and a pinch of gochugaru for dumplings and pancakes
- **Low-sodium options:** labeled 저염 (low salt) for those watching salt intake
- **Seasoned soy sauce:** some brands sell soy sauce pre-mixed for specific dishes, like bulgogi or noodle soups

## How to use each one

Use **jin-ganjang** for bulgogi marinade, braised potatoes, braised fish, japchae and stir-fries. Use **guk-ganjang** in miyeok-guk, bean sprout soup and to season namul, starting with small amounts. Use **yangjo-ganjang** to make dipping sauces for dumplings and pancakes, or salad dressings with vinegar and sesame oil. When a recipe just says "soy sauce" without naming a type, jin-ganjang or a brewed soy sauce is almost always the right choice; guk-ganjang is usually named specifically because it's so much saltier.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What soy sauce should I buy for Korean cooking?**
Jin-ganjang is the most versatile first choice for braises, marinades and stir-fries.

**What is guk-ganjang?**
Soup soy sauce: lighter in color but saltier, used to season soups and namul.

**Can I use Japanese soy sauce in Korean recipes?**
Yes, as a substitute for jin-ganjang or brewed soy sauce, though flavors differ slightly.

**Does soy sauce need to be refrigerated?**
Not strictly, but refrigerating opened bottles keeps the flavor fresher for longer.
