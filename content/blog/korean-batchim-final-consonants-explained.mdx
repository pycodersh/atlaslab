Most learners get through vowels and consonants in a day or two and then hit a wall. They can read 한국말 letter by letter, but when a Korean says it, it sounds like *han-gung-mal* and nothing lines up.

Batchim is why.

## What batchim is

A Korean syllable block can hold a consonant at the bottom. That bottom consonant is called 받침 (*batchim*), literally "support."

- 가 → 강, 갈, 감, 갑
- 무 → 물, 문, 뭄

The letter is the same one you already learned. The rules it follows are not.

---

## Rule 1: Only seven sounds exist down there

Any consonant can be *written* as batchim. Only seven distinct sounds come out.

| Sound | Letters that produce it |
|---|---|
| ㄱ | ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ |
| ㄴ | ㄴ |
| ㄷ | ㄷ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, ㅎ |
| ㄹ | ㄹ |
| ㅁ | ㅁ |
| ㅂ | ㅂ, ㅍ |
| ㅇ | ㅇ (*ng*) |

So 옷, 있, 낮, 낯, 밭 all end with the same stopped *t* sound. Seven different spellings, one pronunciation.

This is good news for speaking and bad news for spelling. You will hear a word correctly and still not know how to write it.

---

## Rule 2: Batchim is not released

English releases final consonants — say "cat" and there is a small burst after the *t*. Korean does not.

For 밥, the lips close and stay closed. For 책, the tongue goes back and stops. There is no air after it.

Adding an English-style release makes the word sound like it has an extra syllable, and it is one of the clearest markers of a foreign accent.

---

## Rule 3: Batchim moves when a vowel follows

This is the rule that breaks listening comprehension.

When the next syllable starts with ㅇ — which is silent — the batchim slides over and fills that empty slot.

| Written | Pronounced |
|---|---|
| 밥을 | 바블 |
| 한국어 | 한구거 |
| 음악 | 으막 |
| 좋아요 | 조아요 |

Nothing changes in the spelling. Everything changes in the sound. If you learned 한국어 as *han-guk-eo* and then hear *han-gu-geo*, you may not recognize a word you know perfectly well.

---

## Rule 4: Batchim assimilates to what comes next

When the following syllable starts with an actual consonant, the two influence each other.

| Written | Pronounced | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| 한국말 | 한궁말 | ㄱ became ㅇ before ㅁ |
| 십만 | 심만 | ㅂ became ㅁ before ㅁ |
| 신라 | 실라 | ㄴ became ㄹ before ㄹ |
| 좋다 | 조타 | ㅎ + ㄷ merged into ㅌ |

There are formal rules behind each of these, and you can learn them if you want. Most learners absorb them by ear long before they could state them.

---

## Double batchim

Some syllables carry two consonants at the bottom: 없다, 앉다, 읽다, 짧다.

Only one of them is pronounced, and which one depends on the word and on what follows. 읽다 is *ik-tta*, but 읽어요 is *il-geo-yo* — the other consonant surfaces when a vowel follows.

These are worth postponing. They are a small set of common words, and learning them one at a time as you meet them is more efficient than studying the rule.

---

## Why this matters more than it seems

Batchim is the main reason there is a gap between reading Korean and understanding spoken Korean. Learners often conclude that natives "speak too fast." Usually the speed is fine — the sounds have simply changed shape, and the version in your head is the spelled one.

The fix is not more rules. It is hearing words and sentences enough times that the spoken form becomes the version you store.
