Most learners get through the vowels and consonants in a day or two, then hit a wall. They can read 한국말 letter by letter, but when a Korean says it, it sounds like *hangungmal* and nothing lines up. Batchim is why.

## What batchim is

A syllable block can hold a consonant at the bottom. That's **받침** (*batchim*, literally "support"): 가 → 강, 갈, 감, 갑. The letters are the ones you already know. The rules they follow down there are not.

## Rule 1: only seven sounds at the bottom

Any consonant can be *written* as batchim, but only seven sounds come out.

| Sound | Written as | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ㄱ (*k*) | ㄱ ㅋ ㄲ | 부엌 → [부억] |
| ㄴ (*n*) | ㄴ | 산 |
| ㄷ (*t*) | ㄷ ㅅ ㅆ ㅈ ㅊ ㅌ ㅎ | 옷 · 있 · 낮 |
| ㄹ (*l*) | ㄹ | 물 |
| ㅁ (*m*) | ㅁ | 밤 |
| ㅂ (*p*) | ㅂ ㅍ | 앞 → [압] |
| ㅇ (*ng*) | ㅇ | 강 |

So 옷, 있, 낮, 낯 and 밭 all end with the same stopped *t*. Good news for speaking, bad news for spelling: you can hear a word perfectly and still not know how it's written.

## Rule 2: batchim isn't released

Say "cat" in English and there's a small burst after the *t*. Korean has none. For 밥, the lips close and stay closed; for 책, the tongue goes back and stops. An English-style release sounds like an extra syllable, and it's one of the clearest marks of a foreign accent.

## Rule 3: batchim moves when a vowel follows

When the next syllable starts with a silent ㅇ, the batchim slides over to fill the empty slot. The spelling stays the same; the sound changes.

| Written | Said | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 밥을 | [바블] | *babeul* |
| 한국어 | [한구거] | *hangugeo* |
| 음악 | [으막] | *eumak* |
| 좋아요 | [조아요] | *joayo* (the ㅎ drops) |

If you learned 한국어 as three separate blocks, you may not recognize it at speaking speed.

## Rule 4: batchim changes before some consonants

When the next syllable starts with a real consonant, the two sounds affect each other.

| Written | Said | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| 한국말 | [한궁말] *hangungmal* | ㄱ → ㅇ before ㅁ |
| 십만 | [심만] *simman* | ㅂ → ㅁ before ㅁ |
| 신라 | [실라] *silla* | ㄴ → ㄹ next to ㄹ |
| 좋다 | [조타] *jota* | ㅎ + ㄷ → ㅌ |

There are formal names for each rule. Most learners absorb them by ear long before they could state them.

## Double batchim

A few syllables carry two consonants at the bottom: 없다, 앉다, 읽다, 짧다. Usually only one is said, and a vowel after it can bring the other back: 읽다 is said *iktta*, but 읽어요 is *ilgeoyo*. These are a small set of common words, so learn them one at a time as you meet them.

<Callout type="info" title="Why natives seem to speak 'too fast'">

Usually the speed is fine. The sounds have changed shape, and the version in your head is the spelled one. The fix isn't more rules: it's hearing words often enough that the spoken form becomes the one you store. The [pronunciation mistakes guide](/blog/en/k-patto/common-pronunciation-mistakes-korean-learners-make) has more of these.
