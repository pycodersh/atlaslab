Naengmyeon (냉면) is Korea's classic cold noodle dish, made with chewy buckwheat noodles in icy broth or spicy sauce, and milmyeon (밀면) is Busan's wheat-noodle version. Add vinegar and mustard at the table, cut the noodles with scissors if you like, and choose mul (in broth) or bibim (spicy, mixed).

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Naengmyeon:** buckwheat or starch noodles, very chewy, from northern Korean traditions
- **Milmyeon:** wheat-flour noodles, softer, a Busan specialty born after the Korean War
- **Two styles:** mul (cold broth) and bibim (spicy sauce, no broth)
- **Avoid:** skipping the vinegar and mustard; they brighten the broth

</KeyTakeaways>

## Naengmyeon vs milmyeon

| | Naengmyeon (냉면) | Milmyeon (밀면) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Noodles** | Buckwheat or sweet potato starch, very chewy | Wheat flour (with some starch), softer |
| **Broth** | Beef broth and/or dongchimi (radish water kimchi), icy | Beef or pork bone broth with herbs, icy |
| **Origin** | Pyongyang and Hamhung traditions in the north | Busan, created by war refugees |
| **Spice (mul)** | None | None to mild |
| **Spice (bibim)** | Medium | Medium |
| **Price** | Moderate to high at famous restaurants | Usually cheaper |
| **Season** | Summer favorite, eaten all year | Summer favorite |

## How to eat cold noodles

When your bowl arrives, taste the broth first. Then add a little vinegar and mustard from the table, stir, and taste again. The noodles are long and chewy, so staff may offer to cut them with scissors (가위로 잘라 드릴까요?). Eat the noodles with chopsticks and drink the cold broth straight from the bowl.

<Callout type="tip" title="Mul or bibim?">
Mul naengmyeon is refreshing on hot days. Bibim naengmyeon is chewy noodles tossed in a sweet-spicy chili sauce, often served with a cup of warm broth on the side. Many people order one of each to share.
</Callout>

Cold noodle restaurants often serve a cup of warm broth (온육수) before or alongside the noodles. Sip it to warm your stomach before the icy bowl, especially if you order bibim naengmyeon.

## The story behind milmyeon

Milmyeon was born in Busan during the Korean War. Refugees from the north, missing the naengmyeon of their hometowns, couldn't easily find buckwheat in the south. Wheat flour, distributed as aid, was easier to get, so cooks adapted the recipe with wheat noodles and a broth made from beef and pork bones simmered with medicinal herbs. The result became a beloved Busan summer dish in its own right.

Naengmyeon itself has two famous northern styles:

- **Pyongyang naengmyeon (평양냉면):** buckwheat noodles in a clear, subtle beef broth, an acquired taste many fall in love with
- **Hamhung naengmyeon (함흥냉면):** chewier starch noodles, often served bibim-style with spicy sauce or raw fish
- **Busan milmyeon (밀면):** softer wheat noodles, a slightly sweeter and spicier broth, and lower prices

## Where to eat them

Naengmyeon specialty restaurants in Seoul serve Pyongyang-style (subtle, clean broth) and Hamhung-style (chewier noodles, often spicy) bowls. Korean BBQ restaurants also serve naengmyeon as a refreshing finish after grilled meat. In Busan, milmyeon shops are everywhere and often cheaper, popular in summer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the difference between naengmyeon and milmyeon?**
Naengmyeon uses buckwheat or starch noodles; milmyeon uses wheat flour noodles and is a Busan specialty.

**Is naengmyeon spicy?**
Mul naengmyeon isn't spicy. Bibim naengmyeon comes with a sweet-spicy chili sauce.

**Why do they cut naengmyeon noodles?**
The noodles are very long and chewy, so staff cut them with scissors to make them easier to eat.

**When do Koreans eat naengmyeon?**
Mostly in summer, and often as a finish after Korean BBQ, though many people eat it year-round.
