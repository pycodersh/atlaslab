The steam is rising off a tray of red tteokbokki, fish cake skewers are bobbing in broth next to it, and the owner is waiting for your order. At a Korean **분식집** (*bunsikjip*, snack bar) the whole thing takes four lines. Here's what to say, what you'll be asked back, and how to keep the spice level survivable.

## The exchange

**손님 (customer):** 사장님, 떡볶이 1인분이랑 어묵 두 개 주세요.  
*(Excuse me, one serving of tteokbokki and two fish cakes, please.)*  
**사장님 (owner):** 먹고 갈 거예요, 싸 갈 거예요?  
*(For here or to go?)*  
**손님:** 먹고 갈게요! 아, 그리고 덜 맵게 해 주세요.  
*(For here, please! Oh, and please make it less spicy.)*  
**사장님:** 네~ 어묵 국물 먼저 드세요!  
*(Sure~ Have some fish cake broth first!)*

## Polite or casual?

The owner here uses plain polite **-요** speech, which is typical at a friendly neighborhood stall. Some older owners drop into casual speech with regulars, but as the customer you keep **-요** the whole time.

| Casual (friends) | Polite (to the owner) | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 떡볶이 1인분이랑 어묵 두 개 줘. | 떡볶이 1인분이랑 어묵 두 개 주세요. | One tteokbokki and two fish cakes. |
| 먹고 갈 거야, 싸 갈 거야? | 먹고 갈 거예요, 싸 갈 거예요? | For here or to go? |
| 먹고 갈게. | 먹고 갈게요. | I'll eat here. |
| 덜 맵게 해 줘. | 덜 맵게 해 주세요. | Make it less spicy. |

## Grammar breakdown

### 사장님: how to call the owner

**사장님** (*sajangnim*) literally means "boss," but it's the standard, friendly way to address the owner of any small shop, stall or restaurant. Even if you're not sure the person is the owner, **사장님** is polite and always welcome.

### 1인분이랑 어묵 두 개: ordering two things at once

**이랑 / 랑** means "and" when you list nouns. Use **이랑** after a consonant (**1인분이랑**) and **랑** after a vowel (**떡볶이랑**). Dishes are counted in **인분** (servings) with Sino-Korean numbers: **1인분** is read *irinbun*. Single items like fish cake skewers use **개** with native numbers: **한 개, 두 개**. The [counters guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-counters-gae-myeong-jan-byeong) has the full set.

### 먹고 갈 거예요, 싸 갈 거예요?: "eat and go, or wrap and go?"

The owner's question is built from two verbs each time. **먹고 가다** is "eat and then go," so eating in. **싸 가다** comes from **싸다** (to wrap) + **가다** (to go): "wrap it up and go." **-(으)ㄹ 거예요** asks about your plan. You'll also hear the more formal **드시고 가세요?** or **포장이에요?** (Is it takeout?), which our ["for here or to go?" guide](/blog/en/k-patto/for-here-or-to-go-korean) breaks down.

### 먹고 갈게요 and 덜 맵게 해 주세요

**-(으)ㄹ게요** answers a question with your decision: "I'll eat here." For to go, swap in **싸 갈게요** or **포장해 주세요**. **덜** means "less," and **맵게** is "spicily," so **덜 맵게 해 주세요** is "please make it less spicy." The [-아/어 주세요 guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-requests-a-eo-juseyo) covers the request ending.

## What the owner might ask you

| You'll hear | Meaning | What to say back |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **먹고 갈 거예요, 싸 갈 거예요?** | For here or to go? | **먹고 갈게요.** / **싸 갈게요.** |
| **몇 인분 드려요?** | How many servings? | **1인분 주세요.** (One, please.) |
| **매운 거 괜찮아요?** | Is spicy okay? | **덜 맵게 해 주세요.** |
| **어묵 국물 드릴까요?** | Want some broth? | **네, 감사합니다!** |

> **The free broth:**
> The broth that **어묵** simmers in is free with your order, and there's usually a ladle and paper cups right by the pot. When the owner says **국물 먼저 드세요**, help yourself. At street stalls you often eat skewers first and pay after, counting the empty sticks.

## What to actually say

- The whole order: **사장님, 떡볶이 1인분이랑 어묵 두 개 주세요.**
- Eating in: **먹고 갈게요.** (*I'll eat here.*)
- Taking it with you: **싸 갈게요.** (*I'll take it to go.*)
- Less spice: **덜 맵게 해 주세요.**
