## Where the two sets come from

**Native Korean numbers** (하나, 둘, 셋…) are the original Korean words for counting. They're everyday words for everyday quantities, and in practice they stop at 99.

**Sino-Korean numbers** (일, 이, 삼…) came in with Chinese vocabulary, much as Latin and Greek number roots came into English. They're the number system of writing, record-keeping and big figures, so they go as high as you need.

English has a faint version of this: *one, two, three* for counting, but *mono-, bi-, tri-* in words like "bicycle". Korean simply uses both sets every day.

## The rule in one line

**If you could point at the things and count them one by one, use native numbers. If it's a figure (a price, a date, a minute, a floor), use Sino-Korean.**

| You're saying | System | Example |
|---|---|---|
| two beers | native + counter | 맥주 두 병 |
| two cats | native + counter | 고양이 두 마리 |
| three o'clock | native | 세 시 |
| thirty minutes | Sino-Korean | 삼십 분 |
| 30,000 won | Sino-Korean | 삼만 원 |
| May 5 | Sino-Korean | 오월 오일 |

Time is the famous mix: the hour is something you count (one, two, three o'clock), the minutes are a figure on a clock face. So 3:30 is **세 시 삼십 분**, every time. The [full guide to the two systems](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-two-number-systems) has every case in one table.

## What to learn first

Don't try to master both at once. This order covers most daily situations quickly:

1. **Native 1–10 with 개 and 명:** ordering and counting people. See the [counters guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-counters-gae-myeong-jan-byeong).
2. **Sino-Korean 1–10 plus 백, 천, 만:** prices, which you'll need on day one. See the [prices guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-prices-money-eolmayeyo).
3. **Hours native, minutes Sino-Korean:** telling the time.
4. **Months and dates in Sino-Korean.** See the [dates guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-dates-days-of-week).
5. Everything else as it comes up.

<Callout type="tip" title="Two things that catch everyone">

**Native numbers shorten before a counter:** 하나 → 한, 둘 → 두, 셋 → 세, 넷 → 네, so 한 개, not 하나 개. **Money counts in 만 (10,000):** 30,000 won is 삼만 원, literally "three ten-thousands", not "thirty thousand".

</Callout>
