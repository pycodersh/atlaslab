You learn 하나, 둘, 셋. Then you learn 일, 이, 삼. Then you find out Koreans use both, sometimes in the same sentence, and the logic isn't obvious.

It's less arbitrary than it looks.

## Where the two systems come from

**Native Korean numbers** (하나, 둘, 셋, 넷, 다섯...) are the original Korean count words. They only go up to 99 in practical use.

**Sino-Korean numbers** (일, 이, 삼, 사, 오...) came from Chinese, the way Latin numerals came into English. They go up indefinitely.

This is roughly like English having both *one, two, three* and *mono-, di-, tri-*. Two sets, different jobs.

## The rule that covers most cases

**Native Korean for counting things you can see and count.** People, bottles, hours, cups, books, animals.

**Sino-Korean for abstract or fixed numbers.** Money, dates, phone numbers, minutes, addresses, floors, anything above 99.

If you can point at the things and count them one by one, it's usually native. If it's a figure written on something, it's usually Sino.

## The one that trips everyone up: time

Telling time uses **both in a single expression.**

**세 시 삼십 분** — three thirty.

세 (native) for the hour, 삼십 (Sino) for the minutes. Every single time.

The reason fits the rule above: hours are things you count, minutes are a written figure. It feels strange for about a week and then becomes automatic.

## Counters

Native numbers almost never stand alone. They pair with a counter word depending on what you're counting.

- 사람 for people — **세 사람** (three people)
- 개 for general objects — **사과 세 개** (three apples)
- 병 for bottles — **맥주 두 병** (two beers)
- 잔 for cups/glasses — **커피 한 잔** (one coffee)
- 마리 for animals — **고양이 두 마리** (two cats)

English does this too, just less often: *two sheets of paper*, *three head of cattle*. Korean does it for everything countable.

And note the shape change: 하나 → 한, 둘 → 두, 셋 → 세, 넷 → 네 when a counter follows. `하나 개` is wrong; `한 개` is right.

## What to learn first

Don't try to master both systems at once. Beginners get the most out of this order.

1. **Native 1 to 10 with 개 and 명** — covers ordering food and counting people
2. **Sino 1 to 10, plus 백/천/만** — covers prices, which you'll need immediately
3. **Hours in native, minutes in Sino** — covers telling time
4. **Dates in Sino** — months and days
5. Everything else as it comes up

Steps 1 to 3 handle a surprising share of daily situations.

## A note on money

Korean counts in units of 만 (10,000), not thousands. So 30,000 won is **삼만 원**, literally three ten-thousands.

This is a genuine adjustment for English speakers, who chunk numbers in thousands. Prices in Korea are the fastest way to get used to it, since you'll hear them constantly.

## The short version

- Countable things → native (하나, 둘, 셋) + a counter
- Money, dates, minutes, phone numbers, big numbers → Sino (일, 이, 삼)
- Time → hour native, minute Sino
- Native numbers change shape before counters: 한, 두, 세, 네

That's most of it. The remaining exceptions are few enough to pick up as you meet them.
