Korean food uses lots of vegetables, but many dishes hide fish sauce, anchovy stock or small amounts of meat. You can eat well as a vegetarian with a few safe dishes, clear Korean phrases and help from temple food and vegan restaurants.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Hidden ingredients:** anchovy stock, fish sauce in kimchi, and meat in stews
- **Usually safe:** bibimbap without meat or egg, vegetable gimbap, japchae (check), hotteok, temple food
- **Best bets:** Buddhist temple food restaurants and dedicated vegan cafes in big cities
- **Avoid:** assuming a dish is vegetarian because it looks like vegetables

</KeyTakeaways>

## Dishes at a glance

| Dish | Vegetarian? | Watch for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bibimbap** | Often, if adjusted | Ask for no meat (고기 빼고) and check the egg |
| **Vegetable gimbap (야채김밥)** | Sometimes | Ham, fish cake or crab stick in the classic roll |
| **Japchae** | Sometimes | Often contains beef strips |
| **Kimchi** | Usually not | Fish sauce or salted shrimp |
| **Doenjang jjigae** | Usually not | Anchovy stock, sometimes beef or clams |
| **Tteokbokki** | Usually not | Fish cake and anchovy stock |
| **Pajeon** | Sometimes | Seafood pajeon contains shellfish; try kimchi or vegetable jeon |
| **Hotteok, egg bread** | Yes / vegetarian | Egg bread contains egg |
| **Temple food** | Yes | Traditionally no meat, fish, garlic or onion |

## Where to eat

Seoul has a growing number of vegan and vegetarian restaurants, especially around Itaewon, Hongdae and Insadong. Search 비건 (vegan) or 채식 (vegetarian) in Naver Map or KakaoMap. Buddhist temple food restaurants serve elegant meals with no meat or fish, and a templestay includes temple meals too.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain clearly">
"Vegetarian" isn't always understood the same way. Spell out what you don't eat: meat, fish, seafood, anchovy stock, fish sauce. A card or phone note in Korean helps a lot.
</Callout>

## Snacks and convenience store options

Street food and convenience stores can be tricky because labels and broths aren't always clear, but there are reliable picks. **Hotteok**, **roasted sweet potatoes**, **roasted chestnuts** and **fruit cups** are usually vegetarian. **Egg bread** and **corn dogs made with cheese only** work for lacto-ovo vegetarians, though many corn dogs contain sausage.

In convenience stores, check ingredient labels for 돼지고기 (pork), 소고기 (beef), 닭고기 (chicken), 멸치 (anchovy) and 새우 (shrimp). Many plain rice triangles, bread, tofu snacks and salads are safe, while most instant ramyeon soup packets contain beef or seafood flavoring.

- **Vegan bakeries and cafes** have spread in Seoul, especially in Hongdae, Itaewon and Seongsu
- **Temple food restaurants** offer refined set menus without meat, fish or pungent vegetables
- **Apps:** search 비건 or 채식 in map apps to find nearby options

## Ordering

Ask before ordering, be specific, and be ready for a dish that can't be changed because the broth or sauce is made in advance. Staff are usually happy to help, but busy restaurants may not be able to cook a dish separately, so smaller, quieter places and dedicated vegetarian restaurants are often the easiest. A translation app with a written note listing what you avoid saves a lot of back-and-forth.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it hard to be vegetarian in Korea?**
It takes some effort because fish stock and fish sauce are common, but big cities have vegan restaurants and temple food options.

**Is kimchi vegan?**
Usually not. Most kimchi contains fish sauce or salted shrimp, though vegan kimchi is available at some shops and vegan restaurants.

**What is temple food?**
Buddhist cuisine made without meat, fish, and often without garlic, onion and other pungent vegetables.

**How do I say I'm vegetarian in Korean?**
저는 채식주의자예요 (*jeoneun chaesikjuuijayeyo*), but it's clearer to list what you don't eat.
