The textbook answer, "은/는 marks the topic, 이/가 marks the subject", helps almost nobody, because English has no topic marker. Here's a way in that you can feel instead.

## Two images to remember

**이/가 points.** It picks one thing out: *this one*, specifically.

**은/는 frames.** It says: *as for this*, here's what I want to say.

Which form you use is just sound: 이 and 은 after a consonant, 가 and 는 after a vowel.

## Pointing vs framing, in a conversation

| Question | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 누가 왔어요? (Who came?) | 에마**가** 왔어요. | The answer is what's being pointed at. |
| 에마는 뭐 해요? (What's Emma doing?) | 에마**는** 공부해요. | Emma is the frame; the activity is new. |

## A new thing enters with 이/가, then continues with 은/는

**친구가 왔어요. 그 친구는 미국 사람이에요.** (*chinguga wasseoyo. geu chinguneun miguk saramieyo*) A friend came. That friend is American.

The friend arrives as new information with 가, then becomes the frame with 는. You'll see this pattern in every story and most conversations.

## The hidden contrast in 은/는

Because 은/는 frames one thing, it quietly suggests others. **김치는 좋아해요** sounds like "kimchi I like (but…)", leaving a Korean listener waiting for what you don't like. That's why 는 can sound a little pointed where 가 belongs.

<Callout type="tip" title="How to get a feel for it">

Notice rather than drill: when you read or listen, ask why each particle was chosen. Learn fixed phrases whole (**저는 ___예요**, **제 이름은 ___예요**) and the particle comes free. And accept a long tail: even advanced learners sometimes pick the less natural one, and people still understand them.

</Callout>
