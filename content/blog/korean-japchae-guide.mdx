Japchae (잡채) is chewy sweet potato glass noodles stir-fried with vegetables, beef and a sweet soy-sesame sauce. It's mild, colorful and party-friendly, served as a side dish at Korean feasts and as a main over rice.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **What it is:** glass noodles (dangmyeon) with vegetables, beef and soy-sesame sauce
- **Spice:** none; sweet, savory and nutty
- **Where:** banchan sets, holiday tables, Korean-Chinese menus (as japchae-bap), supermarket delis
- **Avoid:** overcooking the noodles at home; they turn mushy and clump

</KeyTakeaways>

## Where you'll find japchae

Japchae appears on Korean tables in many forms. At **banchan-heavy restaurants** and set meals, a small portion often comes as a side dish. On **holidays and birthdays**, it's one of the dishes families make in big batches, alongside jeon and galbijjim. **Korean-Chinese restaurants** serve **japchae-bap** (잡채밥), japchae over rice with a savory sauce. **Supermarket delis** and markets sell it by weight, ready to eat.

Japchae was historically a royal court dish of vegetables, without noodles at all. Glass noodles were added in the 20th century, and today they're the star of the dish.

<Callout type="tip" title="Warm or room temperature">
Japchae is eaten warm or at room temperature, which is why it's popular for parties and lunch boxes. If leftovers stiffen in the fridge, warm them briefly in a pan with a drop of sesame oil.
</Callout>

## Japchae variations

Japchae is flexible, and every family and restaurant has its own version. Some use pork instead of beef, or skip meat and add more mushrooms and bell peppers for a vegetarian version. **Japchae-bap** is japchae served over rice, often with a slightly saucier, savory gravy. **Gochu japchae** (고추잡채) at Korean-Chinese restaurants is a different dish: thin strips of pork and green peppers stir-fried and eaten wrapped in fried buns (꽃빵).

Glass noodles also turn up in other Korean dishes. They're tucked into mandu fillings, sundae (Korean blood sausage), budae jjigae and braised dishes like jjimdak, where they soak up the sauce.

- **Holiday staple:** japchae appears on Seollal, Chuseok and birthday tables
- **Lunch box favorite:** it travels well and tastes good at room temperature
- **Supermarket shortcut:** pre-made japchae is sold by weight in supermarket delis

## Making japchae at home

The trick is to cook each ingredient separately, then toss everything together.

1. **Soak or boil the noodles** until just tender, then rinse and drain. Cut them with scissors so they're easier to eat.
2. **Stir-fry vegetables separately** with a little salt so each keeps its color.
3. **Cook the marinated beef** until just done.
4. **Toss everything** with soy sauce, sugar, sesame oil and garlic, adjusting to taste.
5. **Finish** with sesame seeds and egg strips.

You can make it vegetarian by leaving out the beef and adding more mushrooms or tofu.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is japchae spicy?**
No. It's sweet and savory, seasoned with soy sauce, sugar and sesame oil.

**What noodles are used in japchae?**
Dangmyeon, glass noodles made from sweet potato starch. They're chewy and naturally gluten-free, though check the sauce.

**Is japchae a main dish or a side dish?**
Both. It's a side dish in banchan sets and feasts, and a main dish as japchae-bap over rice.

**Can I make japchae ahead of time?**
Yes. It's often served at room temperature, so it works well for parties and lunch boxes.
