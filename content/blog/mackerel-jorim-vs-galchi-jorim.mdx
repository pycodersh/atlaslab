Jorim (조림) is a Korean braising method where fish simmers in a spicy soy sauce with radish until the sauce reduces into a glossy glaze. Mackerel (고등어) is rich and affordable, while hairtail (갈치) is delicate and more expensive, and both are classic rice companions.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Godeungeo jorim:** braised mackerel, rich and oily, budget-friendly
- **Galchi jorim:** braised hairtail, delicate and sweet, pricier, a Jeju specialty
- **Radish:** soaks up the spicy sauce; many people love it most
- **Avoid:** stirring the pot; it breaks the fish apart

</KeyTakeaways>

## Mackerel vs hairtail jorim

| | Mackerel (고등어조림) | Hairtail (갈치조림) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Fish** | Mackerel, oily and rich | Hairtail (cutlassfish), long and silvery |
| **Texture** | Firm, meaty | Soft, delicate, with fine bones |
| **Flavor** | Rich, slightly strong | Mild, sweet |
| **Price** | Affordable | Higher, especially Jeju hairtail |
| **Where it shines** | Everyday home cooking, set meal restaurants | Jeju and coastal restaurants |
| **Bones** | Easier to eat around | Many small bones to pick out |

Mackerel is one of Korea's most-eaten fish because it's cheap, filling and full of flavor, and it's a staple in school and office cafeterias. Hairtail is a treat: long, silver fish cut into pieces, with a mild sweetness that many Koreans associate with Jeju and special family meals. Both are also commonly eaten grilled, but jorim is the way to enjoy them with a bold sauce and rice.

## How jorim is made

Thick slices of radish line the pot, and the fish goes on top with sliced onion and chili. A sauce of soy sauce, gochugaru, gochujang, garlic, ginger and a little sugar is poured over, plus water. The pot simmers covered, with the sauce spooned over the fish now and then, until the radish is soft and the sauce has thickened.

- **Radish:** goes in first so it cooks through and absorbs flavor
- **Spooning:** baste instead of stirring to keep the fish whole
- **Rice:** the spicy sauce is meant to be eaten with plenty of rice

<Callout type="tip" title="Eating around bones">
Galchi has many fine bones. Pull the flesh off the central bone with chopsticks, and check each piece before eating.
</Callout>

## Other ways Koreans cook these fish

Jorim is only one way to eat mackerel and hairtail. Both are also popular grilled with just salt, which is the simplest and often the best way to taste the fish itself.

- **Godeungeo-gui (고등어구이):** salted grilled mackerel, crispy-skinned, a classic set-meal dish
- **Galchi-gui (갈치구이):** grilled hairtail, delicate and slightly sweet, popular on Jeju
- **Ganggodeungeo (간고등어):** Andong's salted mackerel, preserved with salt for the long journey inland
- **Galchi-guk (갈치국):** a clear Jeju soup of hairtail with pumpkin and greens, surprisingly mild
- **Kimchi godeungeo jjim:** mackerel braised with sour kimchi, a hearty home dish

Other fish are braised the same way, including cutlassfish, cod, saury and pollack, and tofu or potato jorim follow the same method without fish.

## Where to eat fish jorim

Set-meal restaurants (백반집) often serve godeungeo jorim, especially at lunch. Jeju is famous for galchi jorim, served in big shallow pots for groups, sometimes with whole large hairtail. Andong is known for salted mackerel (간고등어), often grilled rather than braised.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is jorim?**
A Korean braising method where food simmers in a soy-based sauce until the sauce reduces into a glaze.

**Is braised mackerel spicy?**
Usually yes, from gochugaru and gochujang, though you can ask for less spice.

**Why is galchi jorim expensive?**
Hairtail, especially from Jeju, is a prized fish with limited catches.

**What goes with fish jorim?**
Rice, banchan and often a light soup. The sauce is meant to be eaten over rice.
