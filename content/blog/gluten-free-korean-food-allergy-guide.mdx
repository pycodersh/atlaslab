Korean food can work for gluten-free and allergy-conscious eaters, but the risks hide in the sauces, not the main ingredients. Soy sauce and gochujang usually contain wheat, kimchi often contains salted shrimp, and sesame is in almost everything.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Hidden wheat:** most soy sauce, most gochujang, many marinades and fried coatings
- **Hidden shellfish:** salted shrimp (새우젓) and fish sauce in kimchi and some soups
- **Sesame:** sesame oil and seeds finish a huge share of dishes
- **Avoid:** assuming rice-based dishes are safe; the sauce decides

</KeyTakeaways>

## Where the allergens hide

Korean cooking builds flavor from a small set of fermented pastes and sauces. Learn those, and most menus become readable.

| Ingredient | Common allergen | Where you'll find it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Soy sauce (간장)** | Wheat, soy | Bulgogi, japchae, jorim braises, dipping sauces |
| **Gochujang (고추장)** | Wheat or barley, soy | Tteokbokki, bibimbap sauce, spicy stir-fries |
| **Doenjang (된장)** | Soy, sometimes wheat | Doenjang jjigae, ssamjang |
| **Salted shrimp, fish sauce** | Shellfish, fish | Kimchi, gyeran-jjim, some soups |
| **Sesame oil, seeds** | Sesame | Namul, bibimbap, marinades, garnish |
| **Frying batter** | Wheat, egg | Fried chicken, twigim, jeon |

Soy is the hardest to avoid: it's in soy sauce, doenjang, gochujang and tofu, the backbone of the cuisine. A soy-free Korean meal is possible at home but very difficult at restaurants.

## Dishes that are usually lower risk

No dish is guaranteed safe without asking, but some start from a better place. Grilled meat without marinade, plain rice and simply salted soups are the easiest.

- **Samgyeopsal (plain pork belly):** grilled with salt; skip the ssamjang and marinated sides
- **Seolleongtang and gomtang:** beef bone soup seasoned at the table with salt
- **Samgyetang:** whole chicken soup with rice, garlic and ginseng; ask about the dipping salt
- **Plain rice, steamed sweet potato, fresh fruit:** naturally free of the big allergens

<Callout type="warn" title="Rice cakes aren't automatically gluten-free">
Tteok is usually made from rice, but some tteokbokki uses wheat rice cakes (밀떡), and the sauce is gochujang. Ask for 쌀떡 (rice tteok) and check the sauce separately.
</Callout>

## Asking at a Korean restaurant

Small restaurants rarely have allergen menus, though some chains post allergen information. Staff will usually check if you ask clearly. A printed card in Korean helps when the restaurant is busy.

<Dialogue title="Checking before you order">
  <Line me who="You" ko="저는 밀 알레르기가 있어요." rom="Jeoneun mil allereugiga isseoyo." en="I have a wheat allergy." />
  <Line me who="You" ko="이거 간장이나 고추장 들어가요?" rom="Igeo ganjang-ina gochujang deureogayo?" en="Does this have soy sauce or gochujang in it?" />
  <Line who="Staff" ko="네, 양념에 간장이 들어가요." rom="Ne, yangnyeome ganjang-i deureogayo." en="Yes, the marinade has soy sauce." />
  <Line me who="You" ko="새우젓은 빼 주실 수 있어요?" rom="Saeujeoseun ppae jusil su isseoyo?" en="Could you leave out the salted shrimp?" />
</Dialogue>

Swap 밀 (wheat) for your allergen: 땅콩 (peanut), 새우 (shrimp), 갑각류 (shellfish), 참깨 (sesame), 우유 (milk) or 달걀 (egg). For packaged food, look for the allergen line on the label: 알레르기 유발물질.

## Cooking gluten-free Korean food at home

At home you control the sauces, and three swaps cover most recipes.

1. **Soy sauce:** use tamari labeled gluten-free, or a certified gluten-free soy sauce.
2. **Gochujang:** several brands sell a gluten-free gochujang; check the label for wheat and barley. For a quick stand-in, stir gochugaru into gluten-free tamari with rice syrup and garlic.
3. **Noodles:** sweet potato glass noodles (dangmyeon) for japchae are naturally gluten-free; most naengmyeon noodles contain wheat.

Recipes that convert easily with tamari include [bulgogi](/kpantry/en/recipes/bulgogi), [japchae](/kpantry/en/recipes/japchae) and [gyeran-mari](/kpantry/en/recipes/gyeran-mari), which needs no soy sauce at all.

<DoDont>
<Do>

- Carry a Korean allergy card with your allergens written out
- Ask about the sauce, not just the main ingredient
- Choose plain grilled meat and salt-seasoned soups
- Read packaged labels for 알레르기 유발물질

</Do>
<Dont>

- Assume rice dishes or tteok are gluten-free
- Forget kimchi can contain shrimp or fish sauce
- Rely on "no spice" requests to remove allergens
- Share banchan you haven't checked

</Dont>
</DoDont>

## Severe allergies and travel

If your allergy is severe, plan as you would anywhere: bring your own medication, know the emergency number (119 in Korea), and keep a translated card that says how serious the reaction is. Shared grills, shared frying oil and shared banchan plates make cross-contact common, so a dedicated meal at your hotel or a self-cooked meal may be the safer choice on busy days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Korean food gluten-free?**
Not by default. Rice and many ingredients are gluten-free, but most soy sauce and gochujang contain wheat, so many dishes do too.

**Is kimchi safe for a shellfish allergy?**
Often not. Traditional kimchi uses salted shrimp or fish sauce. Look for vegan kimchi or ask the restaurant.

**Is japchae gluten-free?**
The sweet potato noodles are, but the soy sauce usually isn't. Made at home with gluten-free tamari, it can be.

**How do I say "I have an allergy" in Korean?**
알레르기가 있어요 (allereugiga isseoyo). Put the allergen in front: 땅콩 알레르기가 있어요 means "I have a peanut allergy."
