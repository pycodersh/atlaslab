The hard part of Korean consonants is not the number. It is that Korean makes a three-way distinction where English makes a two-way one, and English speakers cannot hear the third option at first.

Get that system and the 19 letters organize themselves.

## The shapes are diagrams

Hangul's consonants were designed to sketch what the mouth does. Five base shapes:

- **ㄱ** — the back of the tongue rising toward the soft palate
- **ㄴ** — the tongue tip touching the ridge behind the teeth
- **ㅁ** — the mouth, seen from the front
- **ㅅ** — teeth
- **ㅇ** — the open throat

Everything else is one of these with strokes added. ㄷ is ㄴ with a lid. ㅂ is ㅁ with an opening. ㅈ is ㅅ with a line on top.

You do not have to believe this to learn the letters, but it makes them stick.

---

## The three-way system

This is the part that matters. Take the *k* family:

| | Letter | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| Plain | ㄱ | soft, no puff of air |
| Aspirated | ㅋ | strong puff of air |
| Tense | ㄲ | no air, throat tightened, sharp |

English has plain and aspirated (compare the *p* in "spin" and "pin") but treats them as the same letter. Korean treats all three as separate letters that change meaning:

- 사다 (to buy) · 싸다 (to be cheap)
- 자다 (to sleep) · 짜다 (to be salty)
- 달 (moon) · 딸 (daughter)

Four families work this way:

| Plain | Aspirated | Tense |
|---|---|---|
| ㄱ | ㅋ | ㄲ |
| ㄷ | ㅌ | ㄸ |
| ㅂ | ㅍ | ㅃ |
| ㅈ | ㅊ | ㅉ |

And ㅅ has a tense partner ㅆ with no aspirated version.

**A physical test:** hold a sheet of paper an inch from your mouth. Say ㅋ — it should move. Say ㄲ — it should stay still. Say ㄱ — barely a flutter.

Tense consonants also feel different in the throat. There is a moment of tightness before the sound releases, almost like the start of a cough being held back.

## The rest

**ㄴ, ㅁ** — straightforward *n* and *m*.

**ㅎ** — *h*, but it weakens or disappears between vowels. 좋아요 is pronounced *jo-a-yo*.

**ㅇ** — two jobs. At the start of a syllable it is silent, a placeholder so the block has something in the consonant slot. At the bottom it is *ng*. 아 is *a*; 앙 is *ang*.

**ㄹ** — the one that resists English speakers. It is not the English R. Between vowels it is a quick tap, close to the *tt* in American "butter." At the end of a syllable it is closer to an L. 라면 starts with a tap; 물 ends with an L.

Using an English R here — tongue pulled back, lips rounded — is one of the fastest ways to become unintelligible.

---

## Consonants change at the bottom of a block

A consonant in the final position of a syllable behaves differently from the same consonant at the start.

It is not released. In English, the *k* in "book" gets a tiny burst of air at the end. In Korean, the sound stops and stays stopped. 밥 ends with the lips closed and nothing after.

And many different letters collapse into the same final sound. ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ all become a stopped *t* at the bottom of a block. That is why 옷, 있, and 낮 all end identically when spoken.

This is covered properly in [the article on batchim](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-batchim-final-consonants-explained), since it is really its own topic.

## What to practice first

Do not try to master all three levels at once. Learn to produce the plain and aspirated versions cleanly — ㄱ vs ㅋ — because that difference is closer to something English already does.

Tense consonants come later, and they come from listening rather than from instructions. You need to hear 싸다 enough times that your mouth starts reaching for the right shape on its own.
