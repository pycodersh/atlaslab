부산 appears as both Busan and Pusan; 김포 as Gimpo and Kimpo; the surname 이 as Lee, Yi, Rhee and sometimes I. None of these are mistakes. They come from different systems, used at different times, and the old spellings never went away.

## The two systems you'll meet

**Revised Romanization** has been South Korea's official standard since 2000: road signs, government documents and most modern materials use it. Busan, Gimpo, Jeju, Incheon.

**McCune-Reischauer** was the standard before that and is still used in academic writing, older books, and North Korea. Pusan, Kimp'o, Cheju, Inch'ŏn.

| Hangul | Revised | McCune-Reischauer |
|---|---|---|
| ㅂ | b | p |
| ㄱ | g | k |
| ㄷ | d | t |
| ㅈ | j | ch |
| ㅓ | eo | ŏ |
| ㅡ | eu | ŭ |

McCune-Reischauer uses marks (ŏ, ŭ) and apostrophes for the puff of air. People often drop them, which is how 청주 and 정주 can both end up as "Chongju". Revised Romanization avoids marks altogether, which is why it uses letter pairs like *eo* and *eu*.

## Personal names follow no rule

The official system doesn't apply to names, and people romanize their own however they like.

| Name | Common spellings |
|---|---|
| 박 | Park, Bak, Pak |
| 최 | Choi, Choe |
| 정 | Jung, Jeong, Chung |
| 이 | Lee, Yi, Rhee |

Park and Choi are the most common, and both are further from the Korean sound than the official spelling: 박 has no *r* at all. They stuck because they were on passports long before 2000. So don't work out a name's sound from its spelling: ask, or find it in Hangul.

## Why romanization is a poor long-term crutch

- **Tense sounds look like long ones.** 싸다 becomes *ssada*, which suggests a longer *s* rather than the tight, sharp sound it really is. The [consonants guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-consonants-19-basic-sounds) explains the difference.
- **Sound changes come and go.** Revised Romanization writes some of them (Silla for 신라, not Sinla), but many learning materials romanize letter by letter, which teaches a pronunciation nobody uses. 한국말 is said *hangungmal*.
- **Vowels blur.** *eo* and *o* look related on the page; ㅓ and ㅗ are different sounds that change words.

The deeper problem: romanization gives your brain an English-shaped version of each word to store. Every *annyeonghaseyo* reinforces an English reading, not a Korean one.

## When it's still useful

- searching for something before you can read Hangul
- place names and addresses when you travel
- a one-time hint the first time you meet a word

It stops being useful once you can read Hangul, which takes days, not months.

<Callout type="tip" title="The honest recommendation">

[Learn Hangul first](/blog/en/k-patto/how-to-read-hangul-in-one-day), before building vocabulary. It takes an afternoon or two, and everything you learn afterwards is stored in the right form. Learners who put it off often build months of romanized vocabulary and then relearn every word.

</Callout>
