Korean menus use four main words for soups and stews: guk (국), tang (탕), jjigae (찌개) and jeongol (전골). Guk and tang are brothy soups, jjigae is a thicker, saltier stew eaten with rice, and jeongol is a big hot pot cooked at the table for sharing.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Guk (국):** light everyday soup, served one bowl per person
- **Tang (탕):** usually a richer, longer-simmered soup; often a "special" version of guk
- **Jjigae (찌개):** thick, salty stew eaten with rice, often shared
- **Jeongol (전골):** a large hot pot with raw ingredients cooked at the table

</KeyTakeaways>

## The four types compared

| Type | Korean | Broth | How it's served | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Guk** | 국 | Light, plenty of broth | One bowl per person with rice | Miyeok-guk (seaweed soup), kongnamul-guk (bean sprout soup) |
| **Tang** | 탕 | Rich, long-simmered | Individual bowls, often a full meal | Seolleongtang, galbitang, samgyetang |
| **Jjigae** | 찌개 | Thicker, saltier, less liquid | Often one pot shared, eaten with rice | Kimchi jjigae, doenjang jjigae, sundubu jjigae |
| **Jeongol** | 전골 | Plenty of broth, cooked at the table | Large pot on a burner for the group | Mushroom jeongol, beef tripe jeongol |

The lines blur: some tangs are light and some guks are hearty, so think of these as general categories rather than strict rules.

## How to choose

Pick **guk** for a light, everyday meal, the soup Koreans eat at home with rice and banchan. Pick **tang** for a filling soup meal on its own, like samgyetang or seolleongtang. Choose **jjigae** for bold flavors to eat spoonful by spoonful with rice. Order **jeongol** for a group dinner where everyone cooks and shares from the pot.

<Callout type="tip" title="Jjigae is meant for rice">
Jjigae is salty and intense on purpose. Spoon it over rice or eat it with rice, rather than drinking it like soup.
</Callout>

Most soups and stews come with rice and banchan included, so you rarely need to order sides. Jeongol restaurants often let you add noodles or rice at the end to soak up the last of the broth, much like fried rice after Korean BBQ.

## Other menu words for soups and stews

A few more words help when reading Korean menus:

- **Jjim (찜):** braised or steamed dishes with little broth, like galbijjim or andong jjimdak
- **Jorim (조림):** braised in soy-based sauce until glossy, often fish or tofu
- **Bokkeum (볶음):** stir-fried, like jeyuk-bokkeum (spicy pork)
- **Gukbap (국밥):** soup with rice already in it
- **Juk (죽):** rice porridge
- **Ttukbaegi (뚝배기):** the earthenware pot many soups and stews are served in

Soup is central to Korean meals: a typical home meal has rice, a soup or stew, and several banchan, and many Koreans feel a meal is incomplete without something warm and brothy.

## Ordering tips

Guk and tang are usually ordered per person. Jjigae is often sold per person but cooked in one pot for the table, sometimes with a two-serving minimum. Jeongol is sold by size (small, medium, large) for groups. Ask staff if you're unsure how big a portion is.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the difference between guk and tang?**
Both are soups. Tang is usually richer and longer-simmered, while guk is a lighter everyday soup.

**What is jjigae?**
A thick, salty Korean stew, like kimchi jjigae or doenjang jjigae, eaten with rice.

**What is jeongol?**
A large hot pot of ingredients and broth cooked at the table and shared by a group.

**Which is best for beginners?**
Mild tangs like seolleongtang or samgyetang, or doenjang jjigae for a gentle stew.
