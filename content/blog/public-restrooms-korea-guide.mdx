Korea has clean, free public restrooms almost everywhere, especially in subway stations, department stores and public buildings. In cafes and small buildings the door is often locked with a keypad, and the code is usually printed on your receipt.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Always free:** subway stations, department stores, large supermarkets and district offices
- **Door code:** check the bottom of your cafe or restaurant receipt
- **Toilet paper:** flush it in public restrooms; use the bin only where a sign asks
- **Avoid:** waiting until the last minute in residential areas, where public restrooms are rarer

</KeyTakeaways>

## Where to find a restroom

| Place | Free? | Where it is | Tip |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Subway stations** | Yes | Inside or outside the fare gates | Check the station map for 화장실; some are only inside the gates |
| **Department stores** | Yes | Usually every floor | The cleanest option in shopping areas |
| **Large supermarkets** | Yes | Near the entrance or food court | Emart, Homeplus and Lotte Mart |
| **District offices and community centers** | Yes | Ground floor | Look for 구청 or 주민센터 |
| **Open restroom signs (개방화장실)** | Yes | Shops and buildings that open their restrooms to the public | Shown on Naver Map and KakaoMap |
| **Cafes and restaurants** | Customers | Often down a hallway or upstairs, with a keypad | Buy something first, then use the code |

<Callout type="tip" title="Finding the door code">
Many cafes and restaurants print the restroom code (화장실 비밀번호) at the bottom of the receipt. If it isn't there, look for a note on the counter, or ask staff. Many keypads need you to press * or # after the digits, so try that if the door stays locked.
</Callout>

## Reading restroom signs

Restroom signs use simple Korean, and recognizing a few words saves time in older buildings and stations where English is limited. Men's and women's rooms are sometimes on different floors in small buildings, so check the sign before climbing the stairs.

| Korean | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **화장실** (*hwajangsil*) | Restroom |
| **남자 · 남** | Men |
| **여자 · 여** | Women |
| **장애인 화장실** | Accessible restroom |
| **비상벨** | Emergency call button |
| **고장** | Out of order |

## Toilet paper and amenities

Korea changed its rules on toilet paper in public restrooms, so the habits you may have read about in older travel guides are out of date. Today the default is simple: flush the paper, put sanitary products in the small covered bin, and follow any local sign.

| Question | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Flush the paper?** | Yes in most public restrooms. Bins beside toilets were removed from public restrooms years ago |
| **Older buildings?** | Some still have a bin and a sign asking you not to flush; follow the sign |
| **Is paper provided?** | Usually, often on a large roll near the entrance rather than in each stall |
| **Bidets?** | Common in hotels, homes and newer buildings |
| **Soap and dryers?** | Standard in stations and malls; small cafes vary |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are public restrooms in Korea free?**
Yes. Restrooms in stations, parks, public buildings and department stores are free to use.

**Can I flush toilet paper in Korea?**
In most public restrooms, yes. If you see a bin with a sign asking you not to flush, follow the sign.

**How do I find the nearest restroom?**
Search 화장실 in Naver Map or KakaoMap, or head to the nearest subway station or department store.

**Why is the restroom door locked?**
Small buildings lock restrooms to keep them for customers. The code is usually on your receipt or posted at the counter.
