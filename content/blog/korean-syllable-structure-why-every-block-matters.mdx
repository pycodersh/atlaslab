You learned the vowels. You learned the consonants. And you can still only read Korean one painful letter at a time.

This is the step almost every guide skips. Korean doesn't write letters in a line — it packs them into square blocks, and until you read the block instead of the letters inside it, you are decoding rather than reading.

## The block is the unit

English writes left to right, one letter after another: c-a-t.

Korean takes the same letters and stacks them into a square. ㄱ + ㅗ + ㅁ doesn't become ㄱㅗㅁ. It becomes 곰.

Every Korean syllable is one block, and every block occupies the same square amount of space no matter how many letters are inside it. 이 has two letters. 왕 has four. They are the same width on the page.

This is why Korean text looks so even, and it's why counting syllables is trivial once you see it — you just count the squares.

## Four possible shapes

| Shape | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Side by side | consonant + vertical vowel | 나, 미, 서 |
| Stacked | consonant + horizontal vowel | 고, 두, 소 |
| Side by side + bottom | + final consonant | 안, 밥, 집 |
| Stacked + bottom | + final consonant | 곰, 물, 국 |

That's all of them. Every one of the 11,172 possible Korean syllables fits one of these four layouts.

## The rule that decides the layout

You don't have to memorize which words stack and which sit side by side. The vowel decides.

**Vertical vowels** — ㅏ ㅑ ㅓ ㅕ ㅣ — have a long vertical stroke. The consonant sits to their **left**: 가, 너, 지.

**Horizontal vowels** — ㅗ ㅛ ㅜ ㅠ ㅡ — have a long horizontal stroke. The consonant sits **above** them: 고, 누, 스.

**Mixed vowels** — ㅘ ㅝ ㅚ ㅟ ㅢ — have both. The consonant goes top left and the block fills out around it: 과, 뭐, 의.

The logic is spatial, not arbitrary. The consonant goes wherever the vowel leaves room.

## Why ㅇ exists

A block always needs a consonant in the first position. But plenty of syllables begin with a vowel sound — 아, 어, 오.

Korean solves this with ㅇ, which is silent at the start of a block. It's a placeholder that keeps the square looking like a square.

That same ㅇ **does** make a sound when it sits at the bottom: it becomes *ng*. 앙 is *ang*. So ㅇ is silent on top and *ng* on the bottom, and that trips up nearly every beginner once.

## What this unlocks

Seeing blocks instead of letters changes two things immediately.

**You can find word boundaries.** Korean puts spaces between words, but within a word, blocks tell you where each syllable ends. 안녕하세요 is five blocks — five beats — not ten letters.

**Pronunciation rules start making sense.** Most of Korean's sound-change rules describe what happens when the bottom consonant of one block meets the top of the next. 한국어 is written with 국 and 어 separate, but the ㄱ slides over: it sounds like *han-gu-geo*. You can't see that rule until you can see the blocks.

That's the same reason [final consonants](https://www.atlaslabstudios.com/blog/en/k-patto/korean-batchim-final-consonants-explained) deserve their own study — the bottom slot is where most of Korean pronunciation happens.

## How to practice

Stop sounding out letters. Take a sentence and do this instead:

1. Count the blocks out loud before reading anything
2. Read the blocks as whole shapes, even if you're slow
3. Only break a block into letters when you genuinely don't recognize it

Reading speed comes from pattern recognition, not from faster decoding. You want 는 and 를 and 요 to register as single shapes, the way you see "the" without reading t-h-e.

If your letters still feel shaky, the [vowels](https://www.atlaslabstudios.com/blog/en/k-patto/korean-vowels-all-21-explained) and [consonants](https://www.atlaslabstudios.com/blog/en/k-patto/korean-consonants-19-basic-sounds) come first — block reading only works once the pieces are automatic.
