You know the vowels and the consonants, and you still read Korean one letter at a time. The missing step: Korean packs letters into square blocks, and until you read the block rather than the letters inside it, you're decoding, not reading.

## The block is the unit

English writes letters in a row: c, a, t. Korean stacks the same kind of letters into a square: ㄱ + ㅗ + ㅁ doesn't become ㄱㅗㅁ, it becomes **곰** (*gom*, bear).

Every syllable is one block, and every block takes the same space however many letters it holds: 이 has two, 왕 has four, and they're the same width. That's why Korean text looks so even, and why counting syllables is easy: count the squares.

## The vowel decides the layout

| Vowel type | Where the consonant goes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical: ㅏ ㅑ ㅓ ㅕ ㅣ | to the left | 가 · 너 · 지 |
| Horizontal: ㅗ ㅛ ㅜ ㅠ ㅡ | on top | 고 · 누 · 스 |
| Mixed: ㅘ ㅝ ㅚ ㅟ ㅢ | top left, vowel wraps around | 과 · 뭐 · 의 |

Add a final consonant and it goes underneath any of these: 안, 곰, 관. That gives six layouts in total, and every one of the 11,172 possible Korean syllables fits one of them.

| Layout | Examples | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| side by side | 나 · 미 · 서 | *na · mi · seo* |
| stacked | 고 · 두 · 소 | *go · du · so* |
| side by side + bottom | 안 · 밥 · 집 | *an · bap · jip* |
| stacked + bottom | 곰 · 물 · 국 | *gom · mul · guk* |
| mixed | 과 · 뭐 | *gwa · mwo* |
| mixed + bottom | 관 · 원 | *gwan · won* |

## Why ㅇ exists

A block always needs something in the first consonant slot, but many syllables start with a vowel sound: 아, 어, 오. Korean fills the slot with **ㅇ**, which is silent at the top. At the bottom, the same ㅇ is *ng*: 앙 is *ang*. Silent on top, *ng* underneath: it trips up nearly every beginner once.

## What seeing blocks unlocks

**Rhythm.** 안녕하세요 is five blocks, so five even beats, not ten letters.

**Sound changes.** Most of Korean's pronunciation rules describe what happens when the bottom of one block meets the top of the next. 한국어 is written 국 + 어, but the ㄱ slides over: it sounds like *hangugeo*. You can't see that rule until you see the blocks. The [batchim guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-batchim-final-consonants-explained) covers the bottom slot in detail.

<Callout type="tip" title="How to practice">

1. Count the blocks out loud before reading anything.
2. Read each block as a whole shape, even slowly.
3. Only break a block into letters when you don't recognize it.

Speed comes from recognition: you want 는, 를 and 요 to register as single shapes, the way "the" does in English. If the letters themselves still feel shaky, review the [vowels](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-vowels-all-21-explained) and [consonants](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-consonants-19-basic-sounds) first.

</Callout>
