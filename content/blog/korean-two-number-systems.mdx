Korean has two full sets of numbers, and you need both: telling the time uses one for the hour and the other for the minutes, in the same sentence. The dividing line is clear once you see it.

## Where the two sets come from

**Native Korean numbers** (하나, 둘, 셋…) are the original Korean words for counting: everyday words for everyday quantities. **Sino-Korean numbers** (일, 이, 삼…) came in with Chinese vocabulary, much as Latin and Greek number roots came into English, and became the numbers of writing, record-keeping and big figures.

English has a faint version of this: *one, two, three* for counting, but *mono-, bi-, tri-* in words like "bicycle". Korean simply uses both sets every day.

## The two sets

| | Native Korean | Sino-Korean |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 하나 *hana* | 일 *il* |
| 2 | 둘 *dul* | 이 *i* |
| 3 | 셋 *set* | 삼 *sam* |
| 4 | 넷 *net* | 사 *sa* |
| 5 | 다섯 *daseot* | 오 *o* |
| 6 | 여섯 *yeoseot* | 육 *yuk* |
| 7 | 일곱 *ilgop* | 칠 *chil* |
| 8 | 여덟 *yeodeol* | 팔 *pal* |
| 9 | 아홉 *ahop* | 구 *gu* |
| 10 | 열 *yeol* | 십 *sip* |
| 20 | 스물 *seumul* | 이십 *isip* |
| 30 | 서른 *seoreun* | 삼십 *samsip* |

Native numbers stop being practical around 99. Above that, everyone uses Sino-Korean.

## Which one to use

| Use | System | Example |
|---|---|---|
| things with a counter (개, 명, 잔…) | native | 사과 세 개: three apples |
| hours (시) | native | 세 시: three o'clock |
| hours as a length (시간) | native | 두 시간: two hours |
| age (살) | native | 스무 살: 20 years old |
| money (원) | Sino-Korean | 삼천 원: 3,000 won |
| minutes (분) | Sino-Korean | 삼십 분: 30 minutes |
| dates (월, 일) | Sino-Korean | 오월 오일: May 5 |
| months as a length (개월) | Sino-Korean | 육 개월: six months |
| floors, lines, phone numbers | Sino-Korean | 삼 층, 이호선, 공일공… |

**The shortcut:** if a counter follows the thing you're counting, use native numbers. If you're stating a price, a measurement or a position in a system, use Sino-Korean.

**Before a counter, five native numbers shorten:** 하나 → 한, 둘 → 두, 셋 → 세, 넷 → 네, 스물 → 스무. So it's 한 개, never 하나 개. The [counters guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-counters-gae-myeong-jan-byeong) covers the ten counters you'll use most.

## Time uses both

| Written | Said | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 3:30 | 세 시 삼십 분 | *se si samsip bun* |
| 1:10 | 한 시 십 분 | *han si sip bun* |
| 8:45 | 여덟 시 사십오 분 | *yeodeol si sasibo bun* |
| 12:00 | 열두 시 | *yeoldu si* |

시 takes native numbers, 분 takes Sino-Korean, every time. Say 삼 시 and you'll be understood, and everyone will know you're learning.

## Age

In conversation, age is native + 살: 스무 살, 서른 살, 스물다섯 살. On forms and in the news you'll see Sino-Korean + 세: 이십오 세. The [age guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-age-sal-man-nai) covers asking politely and 만 나이.

## Big numbers group by 10,000

English groups by the thousand; Korean groups by the ten thousand.

| Korean | Value |
|---|---|
| 만 *man* | 10,000 |
| 십만 *simman* | 100,000 |
| 백만 *baengman* | 1,000,000 |
| 억 *eok* | 100,000,000 |

So 30,000 won is 삼만 원, not "thirty thousand". The [prices guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-prices-money-eolmayeyo) shows how to read any price tag.

## What to learn first

Don't try to master both at once. This order covers most daily situations quickly:

1. **Native 1–10 with 개 and 명:** ordering and counting people. See the [counters guide: 개, 명, 잔, 병 and the ten you'll use](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-counters-gae-myeong-jan-byeong).
2. **Sino-Korean 1–10 plus 백, 천, 만:** prices, which you'll need on day one. See [reading prices: 천, 만 and 얼마예요?](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-prices-money-eolmayeyo)
3. **Hours native, minutes Sino-Korean:** telling the time.
4. **Months and dates in Sino-Korean.** See [dates and days in Korean](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-dates-days-of-week).
5. **Age, when it comes up.** See [how old are you in Korean?](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-age-sal-man-nai)
