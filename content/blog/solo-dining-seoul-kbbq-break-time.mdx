Eating alone, called *honbap* (혼밥), is completely normal in Korea, and many restaurants are built for it. The only catch is that BBQ and shared-pot restaurants often require two servings, so check the table below before you walk in.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Easy solo picks:** bunsik (snack food), gukbap (soup with rice), baekban (set meals) and rice bowls
- **Tricky:** Korean BBQ, jjim and hot-pot places usually have a two-serving minimum
- **Break time:** many restaurants close roughly 3–5 pm between lunch and dinner
- **Avoid:** taking a four-person table at a busy lunch; sit at the counter if there is one

</KeyTakeaways>

## Solo-friendly vs two-person restaurants

| Type | Korean | Solo-friendly? | Typical price | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bunsik** | 분식 | Yes | 4,000–9,000 KRW | Gimbap, tteokbokki, ramyeon |
| **Gukbap** | 국밥 | Yes | 9,000–12,000 KRW | Hot soup with rice, built for one |
| **Baekban** | 백반 | Yes | 8,000–12,000 KRW | Rice, soup and a spread of side dishes |
| **Rice bowls (deopbap)** | 덮밥 | Yes | 9,000–13,000 KRW | Many have counter seats |
| **Noodle shops** | 국수 · 칼국수 | Yes | 8,000–12,000 KRW | Kalguksu, naengmyeon, ramen |
| **Korean BBQ** | 고기집 | Often no | 2 servings, 30,000 KRW+ | Some places sell single servings or have solo grills |
| **Jjim and jeongol** | 찜 · 전골 | Usually no | 2-person pots | Braises and hot pots come in shared sizes |
| **Chicken and pubs** | 치킨 · 호프 | Yes | Whole chicken 20,000 KRW+ | Portions are large; take leftovers home |

<Callout type="tip" title="How to check before you sit">
Look for 1인분 (one serving) or 1인 메뉴 (solo menu) on the sign or menu board, and for counter seating. If you're unsure, ask at the door before sitting down. Kiosk restaurants are usually solo-friendly.
</Callout>

## Solo-friendly formats to look for

Some restaurants are designed around single diners. Kiosk ordering, counter seating and partitioned booths all signal that eating alone is the norm, and they're common near universities, office districts and train stations.

| Format | What it looks like | Where to find it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Counter seats** | A row of stools facing the kitchen or a wall | Ramen, rice bowl and gukbap shops |
| **Kiosk restaurants** | Order and pay at a screen by the door | Chains, food courts, station areas |
| **Food courts** | Many counters with shared seating | Department store basements, malls |
| **Solo BBQ** | Individual grills with single servings | A growing number of BBQ shops; search 1인 고기 |

## Timing: break time and peak hours

| Time | What to expect |
| :--- | :--- |
| **11:30–13:00** | Office lunch rush; solo diners may be seated at the counter or asked to wait |
| **15:00–17:00 (roughly)** | Break time (브레이크 타임) at many restaurants; doors locked |
| **17:30–19:30** | Dinner rush; BBQ places fill with groups |
| **After 20:00** | Easier to get a table alone almost anywhere |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it weird to eat alone in Korea?**
No. Honbap is everyday life, especially for office workers and students, and many places have counter seats or single-serving menus.

**Can I eat Korean BBQ alone?**
Yes, at places that sell single servings or have solo grills, or by ordering two servings. Many BBQ restaurants won't serve just one portion.

**What is break time?**
Many restaurants close between lunch and dinner, usually somewhere between 3 and 5 pm, to prepare. Check the hours before you go.

**Where can I eat alone late at night?**
Convenience stores, gukbap shops, chicken restaurants and 24-hour chains are reliable options after 10 pm.
