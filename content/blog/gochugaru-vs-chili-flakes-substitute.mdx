Gochugaru (고춧가루), Korean chili flakes, is milder, sweeter and fruitier than the crushed red pepper flakes you shake on pizza. You can swap in pizza flakes in a pinch, but only at half the amount, ground finer, with a little paprika to restore the color.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Gochugaru:** mild to medium heat, sweet and slightly smoky, seedless flakes that melt into sauces
- **Crushed red pepper:** much hotter, sharp and neutral, with hard seeds
- **Best substitutes:** Kashmiri chili powder, Aleppo pepper, or sweet paprika plus a pinch of cayenne
- **Avoid:** a one-to-one swap with pizza flakes; the dish will be far too hot

</KeyTakeaways>

## Why they're so different

Gochugaru is made from Korean red chilies that are dried, deseeded and ground into soft flakes. The best kind, sun-dried pepper labeled 태양초 (*taeyangcho*), keeps a bright red color and a sweet, fruity flavor. Because it's relatively mild, Korean cooks use it by the tablespoon or even the cup to build color and body. Kimchi seasoning paste gets its deep red from gochugaru, not from heat.

Western crushed red pepper is usually cayenne-type chilies crushed with their seeds. Most of the heat sits in the seeds and pith, so the flakes are several times hotter. They taste sharp rather than sweet, and the hard seeds and skins don't soften, so they float in broth and stay crunchy in sauces.

<Callout type="warn" title="If pizza flakes are all you have">
Use half the amount, grind them to coarse sand in a mortar or spice grinder, add 1 tsp sweet paprika for color and a pinch of sugar for sweetness, and let them soak in the sauce for 10–15 minutes before cooking.
</Callout>

## Where substitutes work and where they don't

Substitutes are fine for cooked dishes where gochugaru is one flavor among several: stir-fried pork, spicy soups, marinades and dipping sauces. Kashmiri chili powder is the best all-rounder because it gives a similar deep red with mild heat.

Kimchi is the hard case. A kimchi paste uses a lot of chili, and the flakes need to hydrate in the salty vegetable juices and cling to the cabbage. Hotter substitutes force you to use less, so the kimchi comes out pale and thin. Paprika-heavy blends get the color but miss the fruitiness. If you plan to make kimchi, it's worth ordering real gochugaru first.

## Coarse vs fine gochugaru

Gochugaru comes in two grinds, and they aren't interchangeable.

- **Coarse (굵은 고춧가루):** visible soft flakes. The default for kimchi, stews, marinades and namul. If you buy one bag, buy this.
- **Fine (고운 고춧가루):** a smooth powder. Used for gochujang, glossy tteokbokki sauce, cold noodle sauce and soups where flakes would look messy.

Packs also show heat. Most gochugaru is mild to medium, labeled 덜 매운 (less spicy). Bags labeled 매운 (spicy) or made with Cheongyang chilies are noticeably hotter. Check the origin as well; bags labeled 국산 (domestic) are Korean-grown and cost more than imported chili.

## Storing gochugaru

Gochugaru doesn't spoil, but it fades. At room temperature, the red turns brownish and the aroma goes dusty within months. Korean households keep it in an airtight bag in the freezer, where it stays bright for a year or more. Use it straight from frozen; it won't clump if it's dry when it goes in.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I use red pepper flakes instead of gochugaru?**
Yes, in a pinch. Use half the amount, grind it finer, and add sweet paprika for color.

**What's the best substitute for gochugaru?**
Kashmiri chili powder at the same amount is the closest for cooked dishes; Aleppo pepper also works well.

**Is gochugaru very spicy?**
Usually not. Most gochugaru is mild to medium, much milder than cayenne-based pizza flakes.

**Should I buy coarse or fine gochugaru?**
Coarse is the everyday choice for kimchi and stews; fine is for smooth sauces and gochujang.
