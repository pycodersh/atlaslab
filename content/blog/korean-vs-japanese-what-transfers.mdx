Japanese speakers learning Korean often describe the same experience: the grammar feels like it was already installed. Sentences come out in the right order without effort, and particles behave the way they expect.

That impression is largely accurate. It is also incomplete, and the parts that do not transfer are the parts that trip people up longest.

## First, a correction

Korean and Japanese are **not proven to be related**. The structural similarities are striking, but no genetic relationship between Koreanic and Japonic has been established to the satisfaction of most linguists. The shared features are likely a mix of long contact and independent development.

This matters practically: the similarities are real where they exist, and there is no underlying system that guarantees more of them.

---

## What transfers

**Word order.** Both are subject-object-verb, with the verb at the end and modifiers before what they modify. A Japanese speaker never has to unlearn English word order, which is most of the battle.

**Particles.** Both mark grammatical roles with small attached markers rather than with position.

| Japanese | Korean | Role |
|---|---|---|
| は | 은/는 | topic |
| が | 이/가 | subject |
| を | 을/를 | object |
| に | 에 | location, time |
| で | 에서 | place of action |

The は/が distinction maps closely onto [은/는 vs 이/가](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-topic-vs-subject-particle), which means Japanese speakers arrive already understanding the single hardest thing about Korean particles.

**Sino vocabulary.** Both borrowed heavily from Chinese, and the words often remain recognisable.

| Meaning | Japanese | Korean |
|---|---|---|
| library | toshokan | 도서관 |
| promise | yakusoku | 약속 |
| preparation | junbi | 준비 |
| free of charge | muryō | 무료 |
| calculation | keisan | 계산 |

This is a large vocabulary advantage — often quoted as tens of thousands of words with predictable sound correspondences.

**Politeness as a grammatical system.** Both encode the speaker's relationship to the listener in verb endings. The specifics differ, but the concept needs no explanation.

**Counters.** Both count things with classifier words rather than bare numbers.

## What does not transfer

**The writing system.** This is the biggest single difference, and it favours Korean. [Hangul is 24 letters learnable in a day](/blog/en/k-patto/how-to-read-hangul-in-one-day). Japanese requires two syllabaries and around 2,000 kanji for functional literacy.

Japanese speakers often find this the easiest part of Korean, and reading fluency arrives fast.

**Consonants.** Korean distinguishes three levels — plain, aspirated, tense — where Japanese has one or two. ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ are three separate letters that change meaning, and this distinction does not exist in Japanese.

**Final consonants.** Japanese syllables end in a vowel or ん. Korean syllables end in seven possible consonant sounds, unreleased. 밥, 책, 물, 집 all end in ways Japanese phonology does not produce.

This is the single hardest pronunciation adjustment for Japanese speakers.

**Sound change rules.** Korean spelling and pronunciation diverge systematically — 한국말 is said *han-gung-mal*. Japanese has far less of this, so it arrives as an unwelcome surprise.

**Rhythm.** Japanese is mora-timed with pitch accent. Korean is not. Carrying Japanese rhythm into Korean is audible immediately.

## False friends

Shared Chinese roots produce words that look identical and mean different things.

**애인 / 愛人** — In Korean, 애인 is a boyfriend or girlfriend, entirely ordinary. In Japanese, 愛人 means a mistress. The mistake is memorable in both directions.

**공부 / 工夫** — Korean 공부 means study. Japanese 工夫 means ingenuity or working something out.

**정말 / 精一杯**-type overlaps are rarer, but the general lesson holds: a shared character does not guarantee a shared meaning, and the drift is usually in the direction of "slightly off" rather than "completely different," which makes it harder to catch.

---

## Politeness works differently

Japanese keigo splits into respectful and humble forms — raising the other person or lowering yourself.

Korean has [speech levels](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-politeness-levels-explained) (합니다체, 해요체, 반말) on one axis and honorifics (시, 드시다, 계시다) on another. The two systems are independent: you can speak casually to a friend while using honorifics about their grandmother.

The concept transfers. The mechanics do not, and assuming they do is a common source of errors for Japanese speakers.

---

## What this means practically

Japanese speakers typically progress through Korean grammar much faster than English speakers do, and stall on pronunciation instead.

So the sensible allocation is the opposite of the usual advice: spend less time on grammar drills and more on listening and speaking, especially final consonants and the three-way consonant distinction.
