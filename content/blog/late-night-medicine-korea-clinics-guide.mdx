When pharmacies close, Korean convenience stores sell a short list of approved household medicines, including painkillers, cold medicine, digestive aids and pain patches. For anything more serious, find a late-night pharmacy or clinic, and call 119 in an emergency.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **24/7 option:** convenience stores stock a small set of safe household medicines
- **Pharmacies:** usually close in the evening; some stay open late or on weekends
- **Clinics:** walk-in, fast, and affordable even without Korean insurance
- **Avoid:** guessing doses; read the box or ask the pharmacist next morning

</KeyTakeaways>

## What convenience stores sell

Convenience stores can sell only a government-approved list of household medicines, usually from a small shelf near the counter.

| Type | Korean | Common brands | For |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Painkiller / fever** | 해열진통제 | Tylenol (타이레놀) | Headache, fever, aches |
| **Children's fever medicine** | 어린이 해열제 | Children's Tylenol, children's ibuprofen syrup | Kids' fever |
| **Cold medicine** | 감기약 | Pancol A (판콜에이), Panpyrin T (판피린티) | Cold symptoms |
| **Digestive aid** | 소화제 | Bearse (베아제), Festal (훼스탈) | Indigestion, bloating |
| **Pain patch** | 파스 | Shinshin Pas (신신파스) and others | Muscle aches |

Sales limits apply, such as one pack per product, and these medicines aren't sold to young children. Check the counter or ask the clerk.

## Pharmacy, clinic or hospital?

| Option | Hours | Cost (rough, without insurance) | Best for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Convenience store** | 24/7 | A few thousand won per box | Mild symptoms at night |
| **Pharmacy (약국)** | Daytime, some late or on weekends | Low | Most minor illnesses; pharmacists advise |
| **Clinic (의원)** | Weekdays and Saturday mornings | Around 20,000–50,000 KRW visit | Infections, prescriptions |
| **Hospital ER (응급실)** | 24/7 | Much higher | Emergencies |

<Callout type="tip" title="Finding an open pharmacy">
Search 약국 in Naver Map or KakaoMap and filter by open now. Pharmacies open late are sometimes marked as 심야약국 (late-night pharmacy). For translation help, call 1330.
</Callout>

## Seeing a doctor

| Step | What to do |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1. Choose a clinic** | Internal medicine (내과) for colds and stomach issues, ENT (이비인후과) for throats and ears |
| **2. Walk in** | Register at the desk with your passport; appointments are rarely needed |
| **3. See the doctor** | Visits are quick; use a translation app if needed |
| **4. Pharmacy** | Take the prescription to a pharmacy nearby, often in the same building |
| **5. Pay** | Card is accepted; keep receipts for travel insurance |

- Keep receipts for your travel insurance claim

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I buy medicine at a convenience store in Korea?**
Yes, a limited list of household medicines: painkillers, cold medicine, digestive aids and pain patches. Stronger medicines need a pharmacy.

**Do I need a prescription for antibiotics?**
Yes. Antibiotics require a doctor's prescription, which you take to a pharmacy.

**How much does a clinic visit cost for a tourist?**
Without Korean insurance, a basic clinic visit often costs around 20,000–50,000 KRW, plus the pharmacy. Travel insurance can reimburse it.

**Do Korean doctors speak English?**
Many doctors read and speak some English, especially in central Seoul. International clinics at big hospitals offer full English support.
