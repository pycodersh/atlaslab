을/를 is the easiest Korean particle to understand and one of the easiest to misuse, because the rule is simple and the actual usage is full of exceptions.

## The basic rule

을/를 marks the thing a verb acts on.

> 김치**를** 먹어요. — I eat kimchi.
> 책**을** 읽어요. — I read a book.
> 한국어**를** 배워요. — I learn Korean.

| After a consonant | After a vowel |
|---|---|
| 을 | 를 |

밥**을** · 커피**를** · 물**을** · 영화**를**

That is the entire mechanical part.

---

## Koreans drop it constantly

In spoken Korean, 을/를 disappears whenever the meaning is obvious.

> 밥 먹었어? — Did you eat?
> 커피 마실래? — Want some coffee?
> 이거 봐. — Look at this.

All natural. All missing 을/를.

This happens far more with 을/를 than with 이/가, because word order already tells you what the object is — it sits right before the verb.

So when *should* you include it?

- In writing
- In formal or careful speech
- When the sentence is long and the object is separated from the verb
- When you want emphasis

Early on, include it. Dropping particles correctly requires knowing when meaning would be lost, and that comes with experience.

---

## The trap: verbs that take 이/가 instead

This is where learners lose points. Some Korean words that translate as English verbs are actually adjectives, and they take 이/가.

| Wrong | Right | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 커피를 좋아요 | 커피**가** 좋아요 | I like coffee |
| 시간을 없어요 | 시간**이** 없어요 | I have no time |
| 한국어를 어려워요 | 한국어**가** 어려워요 | Korean is hard |

The pattern: 좋다, 싫다, 있다, 없다, 어렵다, 쉽다, 필요하다 all take 이/가.

But watch this pair:

> 커피**가** 좋아요. — Coffee is good / I like coffee.
> 커피**를** 좋아해요. — I like coffee.

좋다 is an adjective and takes 이/가. 좋아하다 is a verb and takes 을/를. Same English translation, different Korean grammar.

Same with 싫다 / 싫어하다.

---

## 하다 verbs split in two

Many Korean verbs are a noun plus 하다, and the noun can take 을/를.

> 공부해요. = 공부**를** 해요.
> 운동해요. = 운동**을** 해요.
> 요리해요. = 요리**를** 해요.

Both are correct. Splitting it adds a small amount of emphasis on the activity, and it is what you do when you want to modify the noun:

> 한국어 공부**를** 해요. — I study Korean.

You cannot say 한국어를 공부를 해요 — one 을/를 per clause is the practical limit.

---

## 을/를 with movement verbs

A few verbs of movement take 을/를 where English would use a preposition.

> 버스**를** 타요. — I take the bus.
> 길**을** 건너요. — I cross the street.
> 산**을** 올라가요. — I go up the mountain.

타다 in particular catches people out, because English "get on" suggests a location particle. Korean treats the vehicle as the object.

---

## Emphasis use

을/를 sometimes appears where it is not grammatically required, to push emphasis onto the word:

> 잠**을** 못 잤어요. — I couldn't sleep.

This is worth recognising rather than producing.

---

## The short version

- Marks the object, goes right before the verb
- 을 after a consonant, 를 after a vowel
- Dropped constantly in speech, kept in writing
- 좋다 / 있다 / 없다 / 어렵다 take 이/가, not 을/를
- 좋아하다 takes 을/를

The last two lines cause more errors than everything else combined, and they are worth memorising as a pair rather than as a rule.
