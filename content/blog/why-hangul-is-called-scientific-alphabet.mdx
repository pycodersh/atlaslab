Most writing systems weren't designed; they drifted from pictures to symbols to letters over thousands of years. Hangul is one of the few exceptions: created on purpose, by a known person, for a stated reason, in a documented year.

## The problem it was built to solve

Before Hangul, Korean was written with Chinese characters. The two languages are unrelated and their grammar is completely different, so this worked badly and took years to learn; reading and writing stayed mostly with the elite.

King Sejong created the new script in 1443 and published it in 1446 as **훈민정음** (*Hunminjeongeum*, "the correct sounds for teaching the people"). His preface says ordinary people had things to say but no way to write them down. The scholars' postscript to the 1446 explanatory edition adds the famous line: a wise person can learn it in a morning, a slow one in ten days.

## Consonants are diagrams of the mouth

| Letter | What it shows | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| ㄱ | the back of the tongue rising | *g / k* |
| ㄴ | the tongue tip behind the teeth | *n* |
| ㅁ | the mouth | *m* |
| ㅅ | a tooth | *s* |
| ㅇ | the open throat | silent / *ng* |

Say the sounds while looking at the letters: ㄱ and ㄴ in particular are close to cross-section drawings.

## Strokes carry meaning

In most alphabets a letter's shape says nothing about its sound. In Hangul, similar sounds look similar, and the difference is a consistent visual change.

| Rule | Letters | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| add a stroke | ㄱ→ㅋ · ㄷ→ㅌ · ㅂ→ㅍ · ㅈ→ㅊ | a puff of air |
| double the letter | ㄱ→ㄲ · ㄷ→ㄸ · ㅂ→ㅃ · ㅅ→ㅆ · ㅈ→ㅉ | a tense sound |

So you're not memorizing 19 unrelated shapes: you're learning five diagrams and two rules. Scripts where the shape encodes how a sound is made are called *featural*, and Hangul is the standard example. The [consonants guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-consonants-19-basic-sounds) shows the full set.

## Vowels from three marks

The original vowels used three elements: a dot for heaven, a horizontal line for earth, a vertical line for a person. The dot became a short stroke in modern print, but the logic survives: ㅏ has the stroke to the right, ㅓ to the left, ㅗ above, ㅜ below. A second stroke adds a *y*: ㅏ → ㅑ, ㅜ → ㅠ.

## Syllables in blocks

Hangul groups letters into square syllable blocks: 한 is three letters in one. That suited text written alongside square Chinese characters, and it has a reading benefit too: grammar endings attach to the ends of words, and blocks make them easy to spot. The [syllable structure guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-syllable-structure-why-every-block-matters) shows how the blocks work.

## Where the praise goes too far

- **Spelling and sound don't always match.** 한국말 is written with ㄱ and said with *ng*; 좋아요 loses its ㅎ. Spelling keeps the underlying form and speech applies sound rules.
- **Homophones are common.** Many words borrowed from Chinese sound alike, which is one reason Chinese characters still appear now and then in legal and academic writing.
- **UNESCO never named it the best alphabet.** What exists is the UNESCO King Sejong Literacy Prize, for literacy work, and the 1446 manuscript's place in the Memory of the World register. Both are real; neither ranks alphabets.

<Callout type="tip" title="What this means for learners">

Hangul rewards understanding the system over memorizing items. Learn the five base shapes and the two stroke rules, and the other letters arrive almost for free.

</Callout>
