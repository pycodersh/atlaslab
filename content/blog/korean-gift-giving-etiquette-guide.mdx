Gifts matter in Korea, from fruit for a host family to cash envelopes at weddings. Give and receive with two hands, choose a gift that suits the occasion, and avoid a few classic taboos like sets of four.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Two hands:** give and receive gifts with both hands, or the right hand supported by the left
- **Visiting a home:** fruit, drinks or a nice snack box are safe choices
- **Weddings and funerals:** cash in an envelope is the norm
- **Avoid:** sets of four, and writing someone's name in red ink

</KeyTakeaways>

## Good gifts by occasion

| Occasion | Good gifts | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Visiting a home** | Fruit, juice, snacks, a cake | Bring something; don't arrive empty-handed |
| **Housewarming (집들이)** | Toilet paper, laundry detergent | Symbolize things going smoothly and prosperity bubbling up |
| **Chuseok and Seollal** | Gift sets: fruit, hanwoo beef, Spam, oils, health products | Huge gift-set season in stores |
| **Birthday** | Small personal gifts, cake, gift vouchers | Mobile gift vouchers via KakaoTalk are common |
| **Business** | Quality items from your home country, sweets | Modest value; avoid anything too expensive |

## Cash gifts

| Event | Envelope | Typical amounts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Wedding** | White envelope, name written on it | Often 50,000–100,000 KRW or more depending on closeness |
| **Funeral** | White envelope | Similar ranges; given at the reception desk |
| **First birthday (돌잔치)** | Envelope or gold ring | Depends on closeness |
| **Seollal (sebaetdon)** | New notes in an envelope | Given by elders to children |

Many people choose odd-numbered amounts (such as 30,000 or 50,000 KRW) for weddings, though customs vary by family.

<Callout type="warn" title="Gifts to avoid">
Sets of four (four sounds like the word for death), knives or scissors (seen as cutting ties), and shoes for a partner (a superstition that they'll walk away) are traditional taboos. Writing a living person's name in red ink is also avoided.
</Callout>

## Gift seasons in Korea

Gift-giving peaks around the two big family holidays and a few special days. Stores fill with gift sets weeks in advance.

| Occasion | When | Typical gifts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Seollal** | Lunar New Year (Jan–Feb) | Gift sets, sebaetdon for children |
| **Chuseok** | Harvest festival (Sep–Oct) | Fruit, beef, oils, health products |
| **Parents' Day** | May 8 | Carnations, cash, health products |
| **Teachers' Day** | May 15 | Small tokens; strict limits on value apply in schools |
| **Pepero Day** | November 11 | Pepero stick snacks for friends |
| **Valentine's / White Day** | Feb 14 / Mar 14 | Chocolate, then candy in return |

## Giving and receiving

| Step | Etiquette |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Handing over** | Use two hands and a slight bow |
| **Saying something modest** | "It's nothing much" (별거 아니에요) is a common humble phrase |
| **Opening** | Many people don't open gifts in front of the giver; follow their lead |
| **Receiving** | Accept with two hands and thank them |
| **Returning the favor** | Gifts are often reciprocated on a later occasion |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What should I bring to a Korean home?**
Fruit, juice, a box of snacks or a cake are safe choices. Something from your home country is a nice touch.

**How much cash should I give at a Korean wedding?**
It depends on how close you are. Many guests give 50,000–100,000 KRW, more for close friends and family.

**Why is the number four unlucky?**
The Korean word for four (사) sounds like the word for death, so sets of four are avoided.

**Should I open a gift right away?**
Many Koreans wait until later. Follow the giver's lead, and thank them either way.
