A heavy box, a parent who can't lift it, and a child who wants to try. The scene is the same anywhere. The Korean around it shows two things worth knowing: how families talk to each other, and how that changes when you offer help to an adult.

## The exchange

**A:** 와, 상자가 너무 무겁네! 엄마는 못 들겠어!  
*(Wow, this box is so heavy! Mommy can't lift it!)*  
**B:** 내가 들어볼게! 영차!  
*(Let me try! Heave-ho!)*  
**A:** 번쩍 들었네! 와, 우리 아기 힘세다!  
*(You lifted it right up! Wow, you are so strong, sweetie!)*

Every line here is casual speech (반말), which is normal between a parent and a young child. Said to an adult you don't know well, the same words would sound rude. The polite versions are further down.

## Why "엄마는", not "I"

Korean parents talk about themselves in the third person when speaking to their children: **엄마는 못 들겠어** is literally "Mom can't lift it." Dads do the same with **아빠**. It sounds warm, not strange, and you'll hear it in every playground.

Other adults use the same words for parents: a neighbor might call a mother **민준이 엄마** ("Minjun's mom") rather than by her own name.

## Words in the exchange

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 무겁네! | mugeomne! | It's heavy! (a surprised reaction) |
| 못 들겠어 | mot deulgesseo | I can't lift it |
| 들어볼게 | deureobolge | I'll try lifting it |
| 영차! | yeongcha! | Heave-ho! (also 으쌰!) |
| 번쩍 | beonjjeok | in one quick motion |
| 우리 아기 | uri agi | sweetie, literally "our baby" |
| 힘세다! | himseda! | You're strong! |

**-네** marks a reaction to something you've just noticed: 무겁네, 들었네. **못 ~겠어** means "I can't (manage to) ~": swap the verb for 못 먹겠어 (I can't eat it), 못 걷겠어 (I can't walk anymore).

**우리 아기** suits toddlers. With an older child, parents use the name plus 이 after a consonant: **우리 민준이 힘세다!**

## Polite or casual?

| Casual (family, children) | Polite (adults, strangers) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 무겁네! | 무겁네요! | It's heavy! |
| 못 들겠어. | 못 들겠어요. | I can't lift it. |
| 내가 들어볼게. | 제가 들어 볼게요. | Let me try lifting it. |
| 힘세다! | 힘이 세시네요! | You're strong! |

Note **내가 → 제가**: the polite "I" is humbler, not just longer.

## Offering help to an adult

This is the version you're most likely to need on a trip: someone older struggling with a suitcase, a box, or bags on the subway stairs.

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 도와드릴까요? | dowadeurilkkayo? | Shall I help you? |
| 제가 들어 드릴게요. | jega deureo deurilgeyo | Let me carry it for you. |
| 같이 들어요. | gachi deureoyo | Let's lift it together. |
| 하나, 둘, 셋! | hana, dul, set! | One, two, three! |
| 조심하세요. | josimhaseyo | Careful. |

**들어 볼게요** means "I'll try lifting it." **들어 드릴게요** means "I'll lift it *for you*", and the 드리다 makes it a respectful offer. For helping older people on public transport, see [giving up your seat on a Seoul bus](/blog/en/k-patto/giving-seat-elderly-korea-bus).

<Callout type="tip" title="What you'll hear back">

**감사합니다!** (thank you), **괜찮아요, 제가 할게요** (it's fine, I'll do it), or, if they accept, **아이고, 고마워요** (oh, thank you, usually from an older person). Don't push a second time if they say 괜찮아요.

</Callout>

## Praise for children

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 잘했어! | jalhaesseo! | Well done! |
| 최고야! | choegoya! | You're the best! |
| 대단하다! | daedanhada! | That's amazing! |
| 역시! | yeoksi! | I knew you could! |

All four are casual, for children and close friends only.

## What to actually say

- Something is heavier than you expected: **와, 무겁네요!**
- Offering help to an adult: **제가 들어 드릴게요.**
- Lifting together: **같이 들어요. 하나, 둘, 셋!**
- Praising a child: **우리 [이름] 힘세다!**
