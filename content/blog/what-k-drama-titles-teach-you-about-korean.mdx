English K-drama titles are marketing, not translation. *Crash Landing on You* and *What's Wrong with Secretary Kim* say almost nothing about what the Korean titles say.

Which makes the originals unusually good study material. They are short, you already know the story, and each one is doing a specific grammatical job.

## 의: the possessive that stacks

**사랑의 불시착** — literally *love's emergency landing*.

의 links two nouns, roughly like English *of* or *'s*. It is extremely common in titles because it packs a relationship into one syllable.

- 태양의 후예 — *descendants of the sun*
- 눈물의 여왕 — *queen of tears*

A thing worth knowing: 의 is pronounced 에 in this position. 사랑의 sounds like *sarang-e*, not *sarang-ui*. Almost nobody pronounces it as written.

In everyday speech, 의 is often dropped entirely — 우리 집, not 우리의 집. It survives in titles because it sounds literary.

## 에서 온: the relative clause, backwards

**별에서 온 그대** — *the you who came from a star*.

English puts descriptions after the noun: *the man who came from a star*. Korean puts them before: 별에서 온 그대.

Breaking it down:

- 별에서 — from a star
- 온 — came (modifying form of 오다)
- 그대 — you (literary)

That 온 is the key piece. Verbs turn into noun-modifiers by changing their ending, and then sit in front of what they describe. Every long Korean sentence uses this, and drama titles are the shortest place to see it clearly.

## 을까: wondering out loud

**김비서가 왜 그럴까** — *why is Secretary Kim like that, I wonder*.

을까 / ㄹ까 marks a question you are asking yourself rather than someone else. The English title turned it into *What's Wrong with Secretary Kim*, which loses the musing quality entirely.

You use this constantly in real speech:

- 뭐 먹을까? — What should we eat?
- 갈까? — Shall we go?

---

## 하라: the command form nobody speaks

**응답하라 1988** — *respond, 1988*.

하라 is an imperative form used in writing, slogans, and headlines — not in conversation. If you told a friend 응답하라 it would sound like you were quoting a poster.

Spoken imperatives are 하세요 (polite) or 해 (casual).

This is worth knowing because it appears on signs and in advertising constantly, and learners sometimes copy it into speech.

## [Konglish](/blog/en/k-patto/konglish-words-when-english-becomes-korean) in titles

**이태원 클라쓰** spells the English word *class* as 클라쓰 rather than the standard 클래스. The deliberate misspelling gives it attitude — roughly like writing "klass."

Korean does this often in titles and brand names, and it is a small signal that the show is aiming young.

---

## Single-word titles carry a lot

**도깨비** is one word, and the English title *Goblin* is a poor fit — a 도깨비 in Korean folklore is closer to a mischievous spirit than to a goblin, and the show trades on that ambiguity.

**오징어 게임** is exactly what it says: 오징어 (squid) 게임 (game), named after a children's playground game. The English title kept the literal meaning, which is unusual.

## How to use this

Next time you finish a drama, look up the Korean title and take it apart. You already know the plot, so you can work backwards from meaning to grammar, which is far easier than the other direction.

Three or four titles will teach you 의, noun-modifying verbs, and 을까 more memorably than a chapter would.
