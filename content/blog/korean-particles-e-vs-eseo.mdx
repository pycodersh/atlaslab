"I'm at the cafe" and "I study at the cafe" use the same "at" in English. Korean splits them into two particles, and one question sorts almost every sentence.

## The one-question test

Ask yourself: **is something happening there, or is something simply there (or headed there)?**

- **에** — a place where something *exists*, or a *destination*. Also used for points in time.
- **에서** — a place where an *action* happens. Also means "from."

| Korean | Meaning | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 집에 있어요.<br />*jibe isseoyo* | I'm at home. | existence → 에 |
| 학교에 가요.<br />*hakgyoe gayo* | I'm going to school. | destination → 에 |
| 집에서 쉬어요.<br />*jibeseo swieoyo* | I rest at home. | action → 에서 |
| 도서관에서 공부해요.<br />*doseogwaneseo gongbuhaeyo* | I study at the library. | action → 에서 |

Look at the first and third rows. Same place, same English word "at," different particle. Being at home is a state, so 에. Resting at home is something you do, so 에서.

## 에: where things are and where you're headed

Use 에 with verbs of being and movement toward a place:

- 책상 위에 책이 있어요. — There is a book on the desk.
- 서울에 가요. — I'm going to Seoul.
- 공항에 도착했어요. — I arrived at the airport.
- 의자에 앉아요. — I sit on the chair.
- 가방에 넣어요. — I put it in the bag.

Verbs like 앉다 (sit), 놓다 (put down), 넣다 (put in), and 도착하다 (arrive) all describe landing somewhere, so they take 에.

**에 also marks time.**

- 세 시에 만나요. — Let's meet at three.
- 월요일에 가요. — I'm going on Monday.

One exception that catches everyone: words like 오늘 (today), 어제 (yesterday), 내일 (tomorrow), 지금 (now), and 언제 (when) take no particle at all. You say 내일 가요, never 내일에 가요.

## 에서: where things happen

Use 에서 when the verb is something you *do* at a location:

- 카페에서 커피를 마셔요. — I drink coffee at the cafe.
- 회사에서 일해요. — I work at the office.
- 시장에서 과일을 사요. — I buy fruit at the market.
- 어디서 만나요? — Where shall we meet?

In speech, 에서 often shrinks to 서, which is why you hear 어디서 and 여기서 constantly.

**에서 also means "from."**

- 한국에서 왔어요. — I came from Korea.
- 서울에서 부산까지 멀어요. — It's far from Seoul to Busan.

## One letter, two meanings

This pair shows why the distinction matters.

| Korean | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 한국에 왔어요. | I came **to** Korea. |
| 한국에서 왔어요. | I came **from** Korea. |

Same verb, one syllable different, opposite direction. At a passport desk or a first meeting, getting this wrong sends the wrong message.

## The tricky ones

**있다 always takes 에, even if you're "doing" something.** 집에 있어요 means "I'm at home," and it stays 에 because 있다 describes existence.

**살다 (to live) accepts both.** 서울에 살아요 is the standard way to give your address. 서울에서 살아요 also appears, and it sounds more like "I live my life in Seoul." As a beginner, 에 살아요 is the safe choice.

**Meeting places take 에서 when the verb is an action.** 카페에서 만나요 (we meet at the cafe) uses 에서 because meeting is something you do. Asking whether someone is there uses 에: 카페에 있어요?

## Common mistakes

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 학교에서 가요. | 학교에 가요. | Going is movement toward a place. |
| 카페에 공부해요. | 카페에서 공부해요. | Studying is an action. |
| 내일에 가요. | 내일 가요. | 오늘, 내일, 지금 take no particle. |

**The quick check:** look at the verb, not the place. Verbs you *do* (eat, study, work, buy, meet) take 에서. Verbs of *being, going, arriving or placing* take 에. For direction ("toward", "by"), Korean uses a third particle: [(으)로](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-particle-euro-by-to-as).
