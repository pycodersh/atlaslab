If you've understood every word of a Korean sentence but not the sentence, this is usually why: the part that tells you *what happened* comes last. English says "I ate" first and adds detail; Korean holds the verb until the end. That one difference explains most of what feels strange about Korean word order.

## The basic shape: subject, object, verb

저는 김치를 먹어요.
*jeoneun gimchireul meogeoyo*: I kimchi eat.

Three pieces, and the verb closes it. Every full Korean sentence ends this way, including questions and negatives:

- 어디에 가요? — Where (you) go?
- 저는 술을 안 마셔요. — I alcohol not drink.

Once you expect the verb at the end, listening gets easier. You stop waiting for it in the middle and start holding the rest until it lands.

## Particles do the work English does with position

Here is the part that actually matters. In English, word order tells you who did what:

- The cat chased the dog.
- The dog chased the cat.

Swap two words and the meaning flips. Korean does not work that way. Small markers attached to each noun carry that job instead:

- [를 / 을](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-object-particle-eul-reul) marks the object
- 이 / 가 marks the subject
- [은 / 는](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-topic-vs-subject-particle) marks the topic — what the sentence is *about*

Because the markers carry the roles, the nouns can move:

- 저는 커피를 마셔요.
- 커피를 저는 마셔요.

Both mean "I drink coffee." The second sounds like the speaker is contrasting coffee with something else, but nobody will misunderstand who is drinking. The verb stays put; everything before it has some freedom.

This is why dropping particles is riskier than getting word order slightly wrong. Native speakers do drop them in casual speech — 커피 마셔? — but they can do that because context is obvious. Early on, keep them.

## Modifiers come before the thing they modify

The second habit to build is that anything describing a noun sits in front of it. English does this with single adjectives (a red car) but flips to the back for longer phrases (the car *that I bought yesterday*). Korean never flips:

- 제가 어제 산 차 — literally "I yesterday bought car"
- 한국어를 배우는 사람 — "Korean learning person"

So a long Korean sentence often looks like a stack of descriptions that suddenly resolves into one noun, then keeps going until the verb. This feels backwards at first and then stops feeling like anything.

## Where learners get stuck

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 저는 가요 안. | 저는 안 가요. | 안 goes right before the verb. |
| 저는 어디 가요? | 어디 가요? | Drop the subject when it's obvious. |
| translating word by word | learning whole phrases | Korean order comes from patterns, not swaps. |

**When listening, wait for the end.** The last two syllables usually decide whether a sentence is a question, a past tense, a suggestion or a refusal. The [negation guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-an-vs-mot-negation) shows where 안 and 못 sit.
