Your Korean friend walks up and asks, **밥 먹었어?** ("Did you eat?"). You haven't, so you get your hopes up for lunch. But they weren't offering. In Korea, asking whether someone has eaten is one of the most common ways to say "How are you?", and taking it literally is a classic beginner mistake.

## The exchange

**A:** 밥 먹었어?  
*(Did you eat?)*  
**B:** 아니, 아직. 왜? 사주게?  
*(No, not yet. Why, are you buying?)*  
**A:** 아니, 그냥 인사한 건데..  
*(No, I was just saying hi..)*  
**B:** 아, 배고파졌잖아!  
*(Ah, now you made me hungry!)*

## Why "did you eat?" means hello

**밥** (*bap*) means cooked rice, and by extension a meal. Asking if someone has eaten is a way of checking they're doing okay. It's often traced back to harder decades when a full meal wasn't a given. Today it's simply warm small talk between friends, family and coworkers, the same way "How's it going?" doesn't expect a full report in English.

So the right answer is short. You don't need to say what you ate, and nobody is inviting you anywhere.

## Polite or casual?

The dialogue is between two friends, so it's all casual speech (**반말**). With a coworker, a stranger or anyone older, switch to the polite forms.

| Casual (friends) | Polite (coworkers, elders) | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 밥 먹었어? | 식사하셨어요? | Did you eat? |
| 응, 먹었어. | 네, 먹었어요. | Yes, I ate. |
| 아니, 아직. | 아니요, 아직이요. | No, not yet. |
| 그냥 인사한 건데. | 그냥 인사드린 거예요. | I was just saying hello. |

**식사하셨어요?** uses **식사** (meal) and the honorific **-시-**, and it's the everyday version for your boss or a friend's parents. You may also hear the very respectful **진지 잡수셨어요?**, which is reserved for grandparents and the elderly.

## Grammar breakdown

### 먹었어?: the past tense question

**먹다** (to eat) + past **-었-** + casual **-어**. The same sentence is a question or a statement depending only on your intonation: rising for **먹었어?**, falling for **먹었어.**

### 사주게?: "so you're buying?"

**사 주다** means "to buy for someone" (**사다** + **-아/어 주다**). The casual ending **-게?** checks what the other person is about to do, usually with a little surprise or teasing: **벌써 가게?** (*You're leaving already?*). Here it's a playful "Oh, so you're treating me?"

### 인사한 건데: "it was just a greeting, though"

**인사하다** (to greet) + **-ㄴ 거** (the fact that) + **-ㄴ데** (soft trailing "but…"). Ending on **-ㄴ데** leaves the sentence hanging, which is a gentle way to correct a misunderstanding.

### 배고파졌잖아!: "now look, I'm hungry"

**배고프다** (to be hungry) + **-아/어지다** (to become) + past tense = "became hungry." **-잖아** means "you know" or "see?", and here it playfully blames the friend: you asked, so now I'm hungry.

## What they might ask (and how to answer)

| You'll hear | Meaning | What to say back |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **밥 먹었어?** | Did you eat? / How are you? | **응, 먹었어. 너는?** (Yeah, you?) |
| **식사하셨어요?** | Did you eat? (polite) | **네, 먹었어요. 식사하셨어요?** |
| **어디 가?** | Where are you going? | **잠깐 편의점!** (Just the convenience store!) |
| **언제 밥 한번 먹자!** | Let's grab a meal sometime! | **그래, 좋아!** (Sure, sounds good!) |

> **언제 밥 한번 먹자:**
> "Let's eat together sometime" works like "We should catch up!" in English. It's friendly, but it isn't a plan until someone names a day. **어디 가?** is the same kind of greeting, not nosiness.

## What to actually say

- Greeting a friend: **밥 먹었어?**
- Greeting a coworker or someone older: **식사하셨어요?**
- Answering and returning it: **응, 먹었어. 너는?**
- If you really do want lunch: **아직! 같이 먹을래?** (*Not yet! Want to eat together?*)
