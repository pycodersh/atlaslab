"Listen carefully" doesn't tell you what you're getting wrong. These are the seven mistakes that come up again and again with English speakers, roughly in order of how much they affect being understood.

<RuleCard badge="Critical rule" title="The pronunciation mistakes that make Korean hard to understand">

  <RuleAvoid ko="밥" rom="bap" note="Releasing the final consonant with a puff of air." warn="Stop the sound: lips closed, nothing after." />
  <RuleInstead lead="Keep the vowels apart, split the three k-sounds, and learn words the way they're said, not spelled.">

## The seven at a glance

| Mistake | Test words | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1. ㅓ = ㅗ | 서다 · 소다 | round your lips only for ㅗ |
| 2. ㅡ = ㅜ | 그 · 구 | spread for ㅡ, round for ㅜ |
| 3. one *k* sound | 사다 · 싸다 | plain, aspirated, tense |
| 4. released endings | 밥 · 책 | stop the sound, no puff |
| 5. English R | 라면 · 물 | tap, or a light L |
| 6. spelling, not sound | 한국말 · 좋아요 | learn the spoken form |
| 7. English stress | 안녕하세요 | even beats |

## 1. Collapsing ㅓ and ㅗ

English has nothing quite like ㅓ, so learners land somewhere in between. Korean keeps them apart: **서다** (to stand) vs **소다** (soda), **벌** (bee) vs **볼** (cheek). ㅗ pushes the lips forward; ㅓ keeps them relaxed and open. If your lips move, you said ㅗ.

## 2. Turning ㅡ into "oo"

ㅡ has no English match, so learners reach for ㅜ. But **그** (that) and **구** (nine) are different words. For ㅡ, spread your lips slightly, as if starting a smile, and make the sound at the back of your mouth.

## 3. Treating ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ as one sound

English has one *k*; Korean has three, and they change words: **사다** (to buy) vs **싸다** (to be cheap). ㅋ has a puff of air, ㄲ is tight with no air, ㄱ sits in between. Hold paper in front of your mouth: it moves for ㅋ and stays still for ㄲ. The [consonants guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-consonants-19-basic-sounds) has more pairs.

## 4. Releasing final consonants

In English, the *k* in "book" gets a small release. In Korean, a final consonant stops and stays stopped: **밥** ends with the lips closed, **책** with the tongue at the back. A release sounds like an extra syllable.

## 5. Making ㄹ an English R

Between vowels, ㄹ is a quick tap, like the *tt* in American "butter". At the end of a syllable it's closer to an L: **라면** starts with a tap, **물** ends with an L. An English R, tongue pulled back and lips rounded, makes 라면 hard to recognize.

## 6. Reading spelling instead of sound

Sounds change where syllables meet:

| Written | Said | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 한국말 | [한궁말] | *hangungmal* |
| 좋아요 | [조아요] | *joayo* |
| 십만 | [심만] | *simman* |

This is the biggest reason learners miss words they already know at speaking speed: they learned the spelling and they're hearing the pronunciation. The [batchim guide](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-batchim-final-consonants-explained) explains the rules.

## 7. Importing English stress

English leans hard on certain syllables. Korean gives syllables much more even weight, and the meaning rides on the ending. Stressing Korean the English way can make an otherwise accurate sentence sound foreign.

<Callout type="tip" title="What actually fixes this">

Rules help you notice mistakes; they don't fix them. What fixes them is hearing a native line, repeating it straight away, and comparing, again and again, on short sentences you understand.

</Callout>
