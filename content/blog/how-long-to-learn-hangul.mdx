Hangul is the easiest part of learning Korean, and it's worth knowing that before you start, because the internet will tell you Korean is one of the hardest languages for English speakers. That's true of the grammar. It isn't true of the alphabet.

## A realistic timeline

**Day 1 to 2 — the letters.** 14 consonants and 10 basic vowels. Most people can recognize all of them in a couple of focused sessions.

**Day 3 to 5 — assembling blocks.** Korean stacks letters into syllable blocks rather than writing them in a line. 한 is ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ arranged in a square. Once you see the pattern, it clicks quickly.

**Week 1 to 2 — reading slowly.** You can sound out signs and menus, letter by letter. Slow, but it works.

**Month 1 to 3 — reading fluently.** Blocks stop being decoded and start being recognized whole, the way you read English words. This part just takes mileage.

So: **reading at all takes days. Reading comfortably takes a few months.** Both numbers are far better than any other East Asian writing system.

## Why it's this fast

Hangul was created deliberately in the 15th century, not evolved from pictures. The design shows.

**Consonant shapes reflect the mouth.** ㄱ is the shape of the tongue at the back of the mouth. ㅁ is the shape of closed lips. Related sounds share a shape with an extra stroke added — ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ are the same family.

**Vowels are built from three pieces.** A vertical line, a horizontal line, and a short stroke. ㅏ ㅓ ㅗ ㅜ are all the same two elements in different arrangements.

**Spelling is close to phonetic.** Unlike English, letters mostly say what they say. There's no *through / though / tough* problem.

## The two things that actually slow people down

Almost everyone hits the same two walls, and neither is the letters themselves.

**1. Similar-looking letters.** ㅓ and ㅏ. ㅗ and ㅜ. ㅂ and ㅁ. ㄹ and ㄷ. These are genuinely easy to confuse at speed, and confusion here makes reading feel harder than it is.

The fix is contrast practice — read pairs side by side (어/아, 오/우) rather than one at a time.

**2. Sound-change rules.** This is the real surprise. Korean doesn't always pronounce what's written, because adjacent sounds affect each other.

신라 is written *sin-ra* but pronounced *sil-la*. 좋다 is written *joh-da* but pronounced *jo-ta*. 한국말 becomes *han-gung-mal*.

Beginners often assume they've learned Hangul wrong when this happens. They haven't. These are regular rules, they're learned gradually, and reading aloud with audio is how they sink in.

## What not to do

**Don't use romanization as a crutch.** Reading *annyeonghaseyo* instead of 안녕하세요 feels faster for a week and slows you down for months. Romanization can't represent Korean sounds accurately, and it prevents your eye from learning the blocks. Drop it as early as you can stand to.

**Don't learn all 40+ letters before reading anything.** Learn ten letters, then read words made from those ten. Application beats completeness here.

**Don't skip audio.** Reading Hangul silently teaches your eyes but not your ears. Every letter you learn should come with its sound.

## A practical first week

- **Days 1–2:** Basic consonants and vowels. Read simple two-letter blocks aloud.
- **Days 3–4:** Blocks with a final consonant (받침). Read real words — 사람, 학교, 친구.
- **Days 5–6:** Double consonants and compound vowels. Read signs and shop names from photos.
- **Day 7:** Read a short dialogue aloud without romanization, slowly.

Slow is expected. Speed comes from months of reading, not from more study of the letters.

## After Hangul

Once you can sound words out, the alphabet stops being the task and exposure becomes the task. The fastest way forward is reading things you actually want to read, with audio alongside, so the sound-change rules settle in without being memorized.
