When the weather turns cold, Korean streets fill with carts selling hot, sweet snacks: syrup-filled hotteok, fish-shaped bungeoppang and roasted sweet potatoes. Look for steam rising from small stalls near subway exits, carry a little cash, and eat them while they're hot.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Season:** roughly late autumn to early spring
- **Price:** usually around 1,000–3,000 KRW per snack
- **Payment:** many carts take cards or transfers now, but cash is still handy
- **Avoid:** biting into hotteok straight away; the sugar filling is scalding

</KeyTakeaways>

## Finding the carts

Winter snack carts gather near subway exits, markets, bus stops and university streets. Bungeoppang carts have become harder to find in some areas, so locals share spotting apps and maps; a Naver Map search for 붕어빵 often helps. Myeongdong, Insadong and Namdaemun Market have plenty of stalls in winter.

<Callout type="warn" title="Hot sugar">
Hotteok filling is melted sugar and can burn your mouth. Let it cool for a minute, and bite from the edge first. Many stalls fold it in a paper cup to hold it safely.
</Callout>

## Hotteok variations

Classic hotteok is filled with brown sugar, cinnamon and chopped nuts, then pressed flat on an oiled griddle until crisp outside and gooey inside. Busan is famous for **ssiat hotteok** (씨앗호떡), cut open after frying and stuffed with sunflower and pumpkin seeds. Some stalls sell **vegetable hotteok** (야채호떡) filled with glass noodles and vegetables, a savory option, and **green tea or glutinous-rice hotteok** with chewier dough.

Bungeoppang has its own debates. Locals argue about whether to eat the head or tail first, and whether red bean or custard is the "real" filling. A smaller cousin, **gukhwappang** (국화빵), is shaped like a chrysanthemum flower and sold by the bag.

- **Busan:** try ssiat hotteok at Nampo-dong's street stalls
- **Seoul:** Namdaemun Market and Insadong have long-running hotteok stalls
- **Convenience stores:** frozen hotteok and bungeoppang are sold for home

## Ordering and paying

Point and say how many. Many carts now accept cards or bank transfers, but having a few 1,000 KRW notes is still the easiest way to pay. Snacks are made in batches, so you may wait a few minutes for a fresh one, which is worth it. Vendors usually hand bungeoppang over in a small paper bag and hotteok folded in a paper cup, and some will tell you to wait before biting. A warm drink from a nearby convenience store makes the perfect pairing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is hotteok?**
A pan-fried pancake filled with brown sugar, cinnamon and nuts, sold hot from street carts in winter.

**What's inside bungeoppang?**
Traditionally sweet red bean paste. Custard (슈크림) is a popular alternative, and some carts offer other fillings.

**When can I find winter street snacks?**
Mostly from late autumn to early spring. Some shops sell hotteok all year.

**Do I need cash for street snacks?**
Not always, but it helps. Many carts accept cards or transfers, while some small carts are cash-only.
