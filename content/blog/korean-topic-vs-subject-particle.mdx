"은/는 marks the topic, 이/가 marks the subject" is correct and almost useless. **저는 학생이에요** and **제가 학생이에요** are both grammatical, and Koreans hear the difference at once. It isn't about grammar roles: it's about *what's new* and *what's being contrasted*. Four situations decide it. (For a shorter, feel-first introduction, see [pointing vs framing](/blog/en/k-patto/eun-neun-vs-i-ga-difference).)

## First, the mechanical part

| After a consonant | After a vowel |
|---|---|
| 은 | 는 |
| 이 | 가 |

선생님은 · 저는 · 선생님이 · 제가

Note that 나 and 저 become 내가 and 제가 with 이/가. Irregular, and very common.

## Situation 1: New information takes 이/가

When something enters the conversation for the first time, it takes 이/가. Once it is established, it switches to 은/는.

> 옛날에 한 남자**가** 살았어요. 그 남자**는** 아주 가난했어요.
> *Once there was a man. The man was very poor.*

This is exactly like English switching from *a* to *the*. First mention introduces; second mention refers back.

If you notice yourself using 은/는 for something you have not mentioned yet, that is usually the mistake.

## Situation 2: Question words always take 이/가

This one is a hard rule and it is the fastest way to get the particle right.

> 누**가** 왔어요? — Who came?
> 지수**가** 왔어요. — Jisu came.

Not 지수는 왔어요. The question asks *which person*, so the answer supplies new information, so it takes 이/가.

Compare:

> 지수는 언제 왔어요? — When did Jisu come?
> 지수는 어제 왔어요. — Jisu came yesterday.

Here Jisu is already known and the new part is the time, so Jisu takes 은/는.

**The test:** whatever answers the question word takes 이/가. Everything already known takes 은/는.

## Situation 3: 은/는 implies contrast

This is the meaning English speakers keep missing.

> 커피**는** 안 마셔요.

This does not just mean "I don't drink coffee." It means *coffee, I don't drink* — implying there is something else you do drink. A Korean listener will hear the implied "but tea, yes."

> 저**는** 괜찮아요. — I'm fine (whatever the others are).

This is why 은/는 can attach to objects, times, and places, not just subjects. It is not a subject marker at all. It is a spotlight that says "as for this one, specifically."

## Situation 4: General truths take 은/는

> 고양이**는** 귀여워요. — Cats are cute.
> 한국 음식**은** 매워요. — Korean food is spicy.

Statements about a category as a whole use 은/는. Using 이/가 here would sound like you are pointing at one specific cat.

## Both in one sentence

This combination confuses people and it is extremely common:

> 저**는** 커피**가** 좋아요. — I like coffee.

저 is the topic — what the sentence is about. 커피 takes 이/가 because 좋다 is an adjective meaning "is good," so literally this is *as for me, coffee is good.*

Same structure:

> 저는 한국어**가** 어려워요. — Korean is hard for me.
> 저는 시간**이** 없어요. — I don't have time.

Verbs and adjectives like 좋다, 싫다, 있다, 없다, 어렵다 take 이/가 on the thing being described, not 을/를.

## When you can drop them

In casual speech, particles disappear constantly.

> 커피 마셔? — Drinking coffee?
> 나 갈게. — I'll go.

This is normal and natural. But dropping a particle removes the information it carried, so it only works when context makes everything obvious. Early on, keep them.

## Common mistakes

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| (누가 왔어요?) 지수는 왔어요. | 지수가 왔어요. | The answer to a question word takes 이/가. |
| 한 남자는 살았어요. | 한 남자가 살았어요. | Introduce something new with 이/가. |
| 커피를 좋아요. | 커피가 좋아요. | 좋다 is an adjective: the thing liked takes 이/가. |

One more to listen for: **저는 좋아요** when nobody asked about anyone else can sound like you're setting yourself apart from the group. The object particle has its own [guide to 을/를](/blog/en/k-patto/korean-object-particle-eul-reul).
