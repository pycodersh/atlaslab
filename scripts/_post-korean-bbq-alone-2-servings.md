You want Korean BBQ. You're travelling alone. Every table you can see through the window has three or four people crowded around a grill, and you start wondering whether walking in by yourself is going to be a problem.

It isn't. But there's one rule that catches almost every solo visitor.

## The two-serving minimum

Most Korean BBQ restaurants require a minimum order of **2인분** — two servings — regardless of how many people are at the table.

This isn't a rule about you. It's about the grill. Firing up a table burner for a single portion doesn't work economically, and one serving of meat cooks and disappears in about four minutes.

So you can absolutely eat alone. You'll just be ordering food for two.

## Asking before you sit

**혼자 먹어도 돼요?** — *hon-ja meog-eo-do dwae-yo?* — "Can I eat alone?"

Say this at the door. You'll get one of two answers:

**네, 근데 2인분부터예요** — *ne, geun-de i-in-bun-bu-teo-ye-yo* — "Yes, but the minimum is two servings."

**네, 괜찮아요** — *ne, gwaen-chan-a-yo* — "Yes, that's fine."

## Ordering two servings

**2인분 주세요** — *i-in-bun ju-se-yo* — "Two servings, please."

**인분** is the counter for portions. 1인분 is one serving, 2인분 is two, 3인분 is three. You'll see it printed next to prices on every grill menu.

The number takes the Sino-Korean form here: 일, 이, 삼. So it's *i-in-bun*, not *dul-in-bun*.

Two servings of 삼겹살 is genuinely a lot of food for one person. Most people can't finish it, and that's normal — nobody will comment.

## Places that don't have the minimum

Not every grill restaurant enforces it.

**Chains with individual grills.** Some newer places have small burners per seat and cater to solo diners.

**Lunch sets.** Many BBQ restaurants serve a 점심특선 lunch menu with single-portion rice dishes. The grill minimum applies to dinner grilling, not to these.

**혼밥 friendly places.** 혼밥 (*hon-bap*) means eating alone, and it's become common enough that some restaurants advertise being suited to it.

If you want to be sure, ask **1인분 돼요?** (*il-in-bun dwae-yo?*) — "Is one serving possible?"

## What arrives with the meat

Ordering meat gets you a whole table of things you didn't order. None of it costs extra.

| Korean | Romanization | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 상추 | sang-chu | Lettuce for wrapping |
| 쌈장 | ssam-jang | Thick dipping paste |
| 소금 | so-geum | Salt with sesame oil |
| 마늘 | ma-neul | Raw garlic to grill |
| 파절이 | pa-jeo-ri | Seasoned scallion salad |

You wrap a piece of meat in lettuce with a dab of ssamjang, and eat it in one bite. That's the whole technique.

## Who cooks the meat

At many restaurants, staff will grill it for you — turning the meat, cutting it with scissors, and telling you when it's ready. At others you do it yourself.

Either way, the phrase you're listening for is:

**다 익었어요** — *da ik-eoss-eo-yo* — "It's cooked."

If you're grilling yourself and unsure, you can ask: **이거 다 익었어요?** — "Is this done?"

## The scissors

Korean BBQ uses kitchen scissors at the table to cut meat into bite-sized pieces. This surprises a lot of visitors, but it's practical — cutting on a hot grill is easier with scissors than with a knife, and no cutting board is needed.

**가위 주세요** — *ga-wi ju-se-yo* — "Scissors, please" — if there aren't any on your table.

## What to actually do

1. Walk in and ask **혼자 먹어도 돼요?**
2. Expect a two-serving minimum and order **2인분 주세요**
3. Don't worry about finishing it
4. Wrap meat in lettuce with ssamjang, eat in one bite
5. Listen for **다 익었어요**

Eating alone in Korea is ordinary. The two-serving rule is about the grill, not about you.
